What Is an Annuity? Types, Fees and Trade-Offs

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 8 min read

General information, not investment, tax or insurance advice. Based on SEC investor guidance at Investor.gov and FINRA. TopMoneyApps does not sell annuities.

An annuity is a contract between you and an insurance company: you pay money in, and the insurer promises payments to you, starting right away or at a future date. The trade-off is that guarantees and tax deferral come with fees, surrender charges that can last 6 to 10 years or longer, and dependence on the insurer's ability to pay.

Key Takeaways

  • An annuity is a contract with an insurance company that can pay you income starting immediately or at some future date, and it is designed for retirement and other long-term goals (SEC Investor.gov).
  • The four main types are fixed, fixed indexed, registered index-linked (RILA) and variable; RILAs and variable annuities are securities regulated by the SEC and FINRA as well as state insurance regulators.
  • Surrender charges on indexed annuities typically last six to ten years or longer, and early withdrawals before age 59½ may also trigger a 10% federal tax penalty (SEC, FINRA).
  • The SEC's own example of an annual base contract fee is 1.25%, which is $3,750 a year on a $300,000 annuity, before any rider or fund fees.
  • U.S. retail annuity sales reached a record $464.1 billion in 2025, up 7%, according to LIMRA (March 23, 2026).

How an Annuity Works

FINRA describes an annuity as a contract in which an insurance company "promises to make periodic payments to you, starting immediately or at some future time."

Annuities are sorted by when income starts:

  • Immediate annuity: income typically starts within one year of purchase, according to Investor.gov.
  • Deferred annuity: your money first goes through an accumulation phase, where it earns returns, and later a payout phase, also called the annuitization phase, when income starts.

If you annuitize, meaning you turn the balance into a stream of payments, FINRA notes that the decision is generally irrevocable.

How Indexed Annuity Returns Are Limited

Indexed products do not pay you the full index return. The SEC's investor bulletin on indexed annuities (July 31, 2020) explains the common features with examples:

The bulletin also warns that contracts commonly let the insurer change some of these features, such as the cap, over time. Investor.gov adds that "no fee" annuities still use these limits, which reduce your return in the same way a direct fee would.

Fees and Surrender Charges

Annuity costs come in layers. Based on Investor.gov and FINRA:

  • Base contract or mortality and expense (M&E) charge: the SEC uses 1.25% a year as an example. On a $300,000 annuity, that is $3,750 a year.
  • Administrative fees: the SEC's variable annuity bulletin gives examples of a flat $25 or $50 a year, or about 0.15% a year.
  • Underlying fund expenses: in variable annuities, you also pay the costs of the funds you choose.
  • Rider fees: optional add-ons, such as enhanced death benefits or guaranteed lifetime income, cost extra.
  • Surrender charges: a charge for withdrawing money during the early years. Investor.gov says the charge often declines gradually over several years. For indexed annuities, the surrender period typically lasts six to ten years or longer; FINRA notes variable annuity surrender periods of eight years or more.
  • Market value adjustments: some contracts adjust your value when you withdraw early; Investor.gov says these adjustments are often negative and come on top of any surrender charge.

Some annuities pay a bonus credit, typically 1% to 5% of purchase payments, according to the SEC. A 3% bonus on $100,000 adds $3,000, but the SEC warns that higher fees and longer surrender periods might outweigh the bonus.

Free look: after you buy, you usually get a 10- to 30-day free-look period to cancel without a surrender charge, according to Investor.gov and FINRA. Read the contract during that window.

How Annuities Are Taxed

  • Tax deferral: you pay no federal tax on gains until you withdraw money, take income payments or a death benefit is paid, according to Investor.gov.
  • Ordinary income rates: gains are taxed at ordinary income tax rates, not the lower capital gains rates.
  • Early withdrawals: a 10% federal tax penalty may apply if you withdraw before age 59½.
  • No extra benefit inside an IRA: if you buy an annuity inside an IRA or 401(k), Investor.gov says you do not get any additional tax deferral, because the account is already tax-deferred.
  • 1035 exchanges: Section 1035 of the tax code lets you swap one annuity for another without paying tax on the gain, but FINRA and the SEC warn that a new contract usually starts a new surrender period, and you may owe a surrender charge on the old one.

The Guarantee Is Only as Good as the Insurer

Investor.gov says an annuity's obligations are subject to the insurer's financial strength and claims-paying ability. FINRA adds that annuities are not guaranteed by the FDIC, SIPC or any other federal agency, though state guaranty arrangements may offer some protection. That is different from a bank CD or a Treasury bill.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an annuity in simple terms?

An annuity is a contract with an insurance company. You pay money in, and the insurer promises to make payments to you, starting immediately or at some future date. It is mainly used for retirement income.

What are the main types of annuities?

Fixed, fixed indexed, registered index-linked (RILA) and variable. Fixed and fixed indexed annuities are regulated by state insurance regulators; RILAs and variable annuities are also securities regulated by the SEC and FINRA.

Can you lose money in an annuity?

Yes, depending on the type. Variable annuities and RILAs can lose value with the market. Fixed and fixed indexed annuities protect against market losses, but surrender charges, fees and the insurer's financial strength can still cost you.

What is a surrender charge?

A fee for withdrawing money from an annuity during its early years. It often declines gradually over several years; for indexed annuities the surrender period typically lasts six to ten years or longer.

How are annuities taxed?

Growth is tax-deferred until you withdraw it. Gains are taxed as ordinary income, not capital gains, and a 10% federal tax penalty may apply to withdrawals before age 59 and a half.

Should I buy an annuity in my IRA?

The SEC notes that an annuity inside an IRA or other tax-deferred account gives you no additional tax deferral. Whether other features are worth the fees depends on your situation; this page is general information, not advice.
