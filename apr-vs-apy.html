APR vs. APY: What's the Difference?

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 7 min read

General information, not financial advice. Based on Regulation DD and CFPB guidance.

APR (annual percentage rate) is the yearly cost of borrowing money, used for credit cards, mortgages and other loans. APY (annual percentage yield) is the yearly return on a deposit account and includes the effect of compounding. Because APY counts interest earned on interest, it is higher than the simple rate whenever interest compounds more than once a year: a 5% rate compounded monthly equals a 5.12% APY. When you save, compare APYs; when you borrow, compare APRs.

Key Takeaways

  • APR, the annual percentage rate, is the yearly cost of credit; for credit cards the APR is the yearly interest rate, according to the CFPB.
  • APY, the annual percentage yield, reflects the total interest paid on a deposit account based on the interest rate and the frequency of compounding over 365 days, as defined in Regulation DD.
  • A 5% interest rate compounded monthly produces an APY of about 5.12%; compounded daily, about 5.13%.
  • A mortgage APR is higher than its interest rate because it also reflects points, mortgage broker fees and other charges you pay to get the loan (CFPB).
  • In November 2026 the FDIC national average savings rate was 0.39%, while commercial bank credit card plans charged an average of 22.36% on accounts assessed interest (Federal Reserve, August 2026).

What APY Means

Regulation DD, the federal rule that carries out the Truth in Savings Act, defines annual percentage yield as a percentage rate reflecting the total amount of interest paid on an account, based on the interest rate and the frequency of compounding for a 365-day period. Banks must use the same method, so APY lets you compare accounts that compound daily, monthly or quarterly on equal terms.

Compounding means you earn interest on your deposit and on the interest already added to it. The CFPB describes compound interest as earning interest on the money you've saved and on the interest you earn along the way.

The formula in Appendix A of Regulation DD is:

APY = 100 × [(1 + Interest ÷ Principal)(365 ÷ Days in term) − 1]

For a standard account, the result is the same as converting a stated rate with its compounding frequency. A 5% rate grows like this:

CompoundingAPY on a 5% rateInterest on $10,000 after 1 year
Annually5.00%$500.00
Monthly5.12%$511.62
Daily (365)5.13%$512.67

These are illustrative calculations, not offers. The gap between the rate and the APY grows as rates rise and as compounding becomes more frequent, but at savings-account rates it is usually a few hundredths of a percentage point. What APR Means

APR is the price of borrowing, stated per year. How much it includes depends on the type of loan.

Credit cards. The CFPB says credit card interest rates are typically stated as a yearly rate, called the annual percentage rate. One card can carry several APRs for different types of transactions, such as purchases and cash advances, and your statement must show each category with a different APR and the balance in each.

Mortgages. Here the APR and the interest rate differ. The CFPB calls APR a broader measure of the cost of borrowing than the interest rate. The interest rate does not reflect fees, while the APR reflects the interest rate plus any points, mortgage broker fees and other charges you pay to get the loan. That is why a mortgage quoted at a low rate with high points can have a higher APR than a loan with a slightly higher rate and no points. For monthly payment figures, see our average mortgage payment guide.

How Credit Card APR Compounds

APR does not show compounding, but credit card interest can still compound. The CFPB explains that many issuers use a daily periodic rate, which is the APR divided by 360 or 365, depending on the issuer. They multiply it by the balance at the end of each day, and that interest is added to the balance, so the next day's interest is charged on it too.

To illustrate: the Federal Reserve reports an average 22.36% APR on credit card accounts assessed interest (August 2026). If a balance at that rate were left unpaid for a full year with daily compounding on a 365-day basis, the effective annual cost would be about 25.05%. This is a simplified calculation that ignores payments, new purchases and fees, but it shows why carrying a balance costs more than the APR suggests. How to Use APR and APY When You Compare

  • Savings: compare APYs, not interest rates, because APY already accounts for compounding. Check whether the APY is variable and whether a minimum balance applies.
  • Credit cards: compare the purchase APR, and also the balance transfer and cash advance APRs if you might use them. If you pay your full balance each month, the APR matters less.
  • Mortgages: compare both the interest rate, which sets your monthly payment, and the APR, which adds upfront fees. The APR is most useful when you compare loan offers of the same type and term.
  • Watch the wording: a bank advertising a savings "rate" instead of an APY, or a lender quoting a mortgage "rate" without the APR, is giving you only part of the picture.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between APR and APY?

APR is the yearly cost of borrowing and does not include compounding. APY is the yearly return on a deposit account and includes compounding over 365 days, so it is higher than the stated rate when interest compounds more than once a year.

Is a higher APY better?

Yes, for savings. A higher APY means you earn more interest on the same balance. For loans and credit cards, you want a lower APR.

Why is my mortgage APR higher than the interest rate?

Because the APR also reflects points, mortgage broker fees and other charges you pay to get the loan, while the interest rate does not include fees, according to the CFPB.

Does credit card interest compound?

Often, yes. Many issuers apply a daily periodic rate, the APR divided by 360 or 365, to each day's balance and add that interest to the balance, so interest is charged on interest.

How do you convert APR to APY?

Divide the rate by the number of compounding periods, add 1, raise the result to the number of periods in a year and subtract 1. A 5% rate compounded monthly is (1 + 0.05/12) to the 12th power minus 1, or about 5.12%.
