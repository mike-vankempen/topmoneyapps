Spousal IRA: How It Works, Limits and Who Qualifies

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 7 min read

General information, not tax or investment advice. Rules from IRS Publication 590-A; confirm your own situation with a tax professional.

A spousal IRA lets a married person with little or no paid income contribute to their own IRA based on their spouse's earnings, as long as the couple files a joint return. For 2026 and 2027, each spouse can put in up to $7,500 ($8,600 at age 50 or older), as long as the couple's combined taxable compensation covers both contributions.

Key Takeaways

  • A spousal IRA is not a special account type: it is a regular traditional or Roth IRA in the lower-earning spouse's name, funded under an IRS rule for married couples who file jointly (IRS Publication 590-A).
  • The IRA contribution limit is $7,500 for 2026 and stays $7,500 for 2027, with a $1,100 catch-up at age 50 or older, so each spouse can contribute up to $8,600 (IRS).
  • A couple's combined IRA contributions cannot exceed the taxable compensation reported on their joint return (IRS IRA contribution limits page).
  • For 2026, a spouse not covered by a workplace plan who is married to someone who is covered can fully deduct traditional IRA contributions below $242,000 of joint MAGI; the deduction phases out by $252,000 ($250,000 to $260,000 for 2027).
  • There is no joint IRA: each spouse owns a separate account and controls its investments, beneficiaries and withdrawals (Fidelity).

What a Spousal IRA Is

Normally you can only contribute to an IRA if you have taxable compensation, such as wages or self-employment income. That shuts out a spouse who stays home, works part-time or is between jobs. The IRS solves this with what Publication 590-A calls the Kay Bailey Hutchison Spousal IRA rule: if you file a joint return and your taxable compensation is less than your spouse's, you can use your spouse's compensation to qualify.

The account itself is an ordinary IRA. It can be a traditional IRA (possible tax deduction now, taxed withdrawals later) or a Roth IRA (no deduction, tax-free qualified withdrawals). Not sure which? Our Roth IRA vs. traditional IRA guide compares them.

Two points people often miss:

  • There is no joint IRA. The IRS says spouses "can't both participate in the same IRA." Each spouse has an account in their own name.
  • The account belongs to the lower-earning spouse. Fidelity notes that the owner is the person whose name is on the account, and that person controls the investments, beneficiaries and withdrawals, even if the working spouse's paycheck funds it.

Who Qualifies

Based on IRS Publication 590-A and the IRS IRA contribution limits page, you can make a spousal IRA contribution if:

  • You are married and file a joint federal tax return. Fidelity notes that couples who file separately are not eligible for a spousal IRA.
  • Your taxable compensation for the year is less than your spouse's (it can be zero).
  • The couple's combined taxable compensation is at least as much as the total you both contribute.

There is no age limit. The IRS says that for 2020 and later there is no age limit on making regular contributions to traditional or Roth IRAs, after the old rule barring traditional IRA contributions at age 70½ was repealed.

What counts as compensation matters. Wages, salary and self-employment earnings count. Publication 590-A says investment income does not, including interest, dividends and rental income. Taxable alimony counts only for divorce or separation agreements executed on or before December 31, 2018.

The limit is shared across all of one person's IRAs. If the lower-earning spouse already has a traditional IRA and a Roth IRA, their combined contributions to both still cannot exceed $7,500 (or $8,600 at 50+) for the year. See every account's limit on our 2026 contribution limits page.

How the Combined-Income Rule Works

Publication 590-A says the most that can go into the lower-earning spouse's IRA is the smaller of two amounts:

  1. The annual IRA limit ($7,500, or $8,600 at age 50 or older, for 2026), or
  2. The couple's total taxable compensation, minus the higher earner's traditional IRA contribution and any Roth IRA contribution made for the higher earner.

Two examples using the 2026 limits, for a couple both under 50:

SituationHigher earner's IRALower earner's IRA
One spouse earns $60,000, the other $0$7,500$7,500 (since $60,000 − $7,500 is well above the limit)
One spouse earns $12,000, the other $0$7,500$4,500 ($12,000 − $7,500)

In the second case the couple could split the $12,000 differently, for example $6,000 each. The total simply cannot exceed what they earned.

Can You Deduct It, or Use a Roth?

Anyone with enough joint compensation can contribute to a traditional IRA, but the tax deduction and Roth eligibility depend on income and workplace plan coverage. These are the 2026 and 2027 ranges for married couples filing jointly, based on modified adjusted gross income (MAGI):

Rule (married filing jointly)2026 phase-out2027 phase-out
Traditional IRA deduction: contributor not covered by a workplace plan, spouse is covered$242,000–$252,000$250,000–$260,000
Traditional IRA deduction: contributor covered by a workplace plan$129,000–$149,000$133,000–$153,000
Roth IRA contribution$242,000–$252,000$250,000–$260,000

Sources: IRS Publication 590-A (2025 edition, 2026 figures) and IR-2026-136 (2027 figures). Below the range you get the full deduction or full Roth contribution; inside it, a partial one; at or above the top, none.

For a typical spousal IRA, the first row is the one that applies: the non-working spouse is not covered by a workplace plan, but the working spouse often is. That spouse can fully deduct a traditional IRA contribution if joint MAGI is under $242,000 in 2026.

If your income is above the Roth range, some couples use a backdoor Roth IRA, which has its own tax rules.

Deadlines and How to Open One

You can contribute for a tax year at any time during the year or up to the due date of your return, not including extensions, according to Publication 590-A. Fidelity notes that is usually April 15 of the following year.

Opening one works like opening any IRA. Fidelity lists the steps as: choose a provider, give the lower-earning spouse's personal details including their Social Security number, pick traditional or Roth, fund it from a bank account and choose investments. The account is opened in the lower-earning spouse's name. Our best apps for IRA investing page compares providers.

Watch out: filing separately ends eligibility. If you are married filing separately and lived with your spouse during the year, Publication 590-A says your Roth IRA contribution phases out between $0 and $10,000 of MAGI, so most couples who file separately cannot contribute to a Roth at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a spousal IRA?

A spousal IRA is a traditional or Roth IRA in the name of a married person with little or no taxable compensation, funded using the working spouse's income. The couple must file a joint tax return.

How much can I put in a spousal IRA in 2026?

Up to $7,500, or $8,600 if the account owner is 50 or older. The limit stays $7,500 for 2027. The couple's combined IRA contributions cannot exceed the taxable compensation on their joint return.

Can a spouse with no income contribute to a Roth IRA?

Yes, through the spousal IRA rule, if you file jointly and your joint modified AGI is below the Roth limit. For 2026 the Roth contribution phases out between $242,000 and $252,000; for 2027 it is $250,000 to $260,000.

Is a spousal IRA contribution tax-deductible?

A traditional IRA contribution for a spouse who is not covered by a workplace plan is fully deductible for 2026 if joint MAGI is under $242,000, partly deductible up to $252,000, and not deductible above that.

Can we open a joint IRA?

No. The IRS says spouses cannot both participate in the same IRA. Each spouse has a separate account in their own name and controls its investments, beneficiaries and withdrawals.

What is the deadline for a spousal IRA contribution?

The due date of your tax return for that year, not including extensions, usually around April 15 of the following year.
