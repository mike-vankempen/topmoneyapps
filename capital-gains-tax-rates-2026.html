2026 Capital Gains Tax Rates and Brackets

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 7 min read

General information, not tax advice. Figures are from IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32; confirm your own situation with the IRS or a tax professional.

In 2026, long-term capital gains are taxed at 0%, 15% or 20%, depending on your taxable income. A single filer pays 0% on long-term gains while taxable income stays at or below $49,450 ($98,900 for married couples filing jointly), and the 20% rate starts above $545,500 ($613,700 joint). Gains on assets held one year or less are short-term and taxed at your ordinary income tax rates.

Key Takeaways

  • For 2026, the long-term capital gains rates are 0%, 15% and 20%, based on your total taxable income.
  • For 2026, the 0% long-term capital gains rate applies to taxable income up to $49,450 for single filers, $98,900 for married couples filing jointly and $66,200 for heads of household.
  • For 2026, the 20% long-term capital gains rate applies to taxable income above $545,500 for single filers, $613,700 for joint filers, $579,600 for heads of household and $306,850 for married filing separately.
  • Gains on assets held one year or less are short-term and taxed as ordinary income at rates from 10% to 37%.
  • Net capital losses can offset up to $3,000 of other income per year ($1,500 if married filing separately), with the rest carried forward.

Short-Term vs. Long-Term Capital Gains

A capital gain is the profit when you sell an asset, such as a stock or fund, for more than you paid. How long you held it decides the rate:

  • Long-term: you held the asset for more than one year. Taxed at 0%, 15% or 20%.
  • Short-term: you held it one year or less. Taxed as ordinary income at the same 10%–37% rates as wages. See the 2026 tax brackets.

The IRS counts the holding period from the day after you buy the asset up to and including the day you sell it. Selling a few days too early can move a gain from a 15% rate to a 22% or 24% ordinary rate.

How the 0% Rate Works: A 2026 Example

Gains "stack" on top of your ordinary income. The 0% rate covers only the part of your gains that fits under the threshold.

Example: a single filer has $40,000 of taxable ordinary income (after the standard deduction) and $20,000 of long-term gains, for $60,000 of total taxable income.

  • The 0% bracket runs to $49,450, so the first $9,450 of the gain ($49,450 minus $40,000) is taxed at 0%.
  • The remaining $10,550 of the gain is taxed at 15%: $1,582.50.

The $40,000 of ordinary income is still taxed at the normal brackets. The gains do not push that income into a higher rate.

Why it matters: people with low taxable income in a given year, such as early retirees or students, may be able to sell appreciated investments and owe no federal tax on the long-term gain. The 2026 standard deduction ($16,100 single, $32,200 joint) is subtracted before the threshold is measured. See the 2026 standard deduction guide.

Exceptions: 25% and 28% Rates

IRS Topic 409 lists three kinds of long-term gain that can be taxed above 20%:

Type of gainMaximum rate
Collectibles, such as coins or art28%
Taxable part of gain on qualified small business stock (section 1202)28%
Unrecaptured section 1250 gain from selling depreciated real estate25%

Higher-income investors may also owe the separate net investment income tax on top of these rates; the IRS covers it in Topic 559.

Capital Losses and the $3,000 Rule

Losses offset gains first. If your capital losses exceed your gains for the year, you can deduct up to $3,000 of the net loss against other income ($1,500 if married filing separately). Any net loss above that limit carries forward to later years.

This is the basis of tax-loss harvesting: selling investments that are down to realize a loss that offsets gains. For the timing rules and other moves before December 31, see our year-end tax moves.

How Gains Are Reported

Most sales go on Form 8949 and are summarized on Schedule D of Form 1040. If you expect a large gain, IRS Publication 505 explains estimated tax payments so you are not underpaid during the year.

Investments held inside retirement accounts such as an IRA or 401(k) follow those accounts' own tax rules rather than the rates above. See the best IRA apps and 2026 contribution limits.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the capital gains tax rates for 2026?

Long-term capital gains are taxed at 0%, 15% or 20% in 2026. Single filers pay 0% with taxable income up to $49,450, 15% up to $545,500 and 20% above that. Married couples filing jointly pay 0% up to $98,900, 15% up to $613,700 and 20% above that.

How are short-term capital gains taxed in 2026?

Gains on assets held one year or less are short-term and taxed as ordinary income at the regular 2026 rates of 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35% and 37%.

How long do I have to hold an investment for long-term rates?

More than one year. The IRS counts the holding period from the day after you acquire the asset through the day you sell it. An asset held exactly one year or less produces a short-term gain.

How much in capital losses can I deduct?

Net capital losses can offset up to $3,000 of other income per year, or $1,500 if married filing separately. Any remaining loss carries forward to future tax years.

Can I pay 0% tax on capital gains?

Yes, if your total taxable income including the gain stays within the 0% bracket: up to $49,450 for single filers, $98,900 for married couples filing jointly and $66,200 for heads of household in 2026. Only the part of the gain under the threshold gets the 0% rate.
