Money Market Account vs. High-Yield Savings Account

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 6 min read

General information, not financial advice. Based on the CFPB and Federal Reserve guidance.

A money market account is an interest-bearing bank or credit union account that adds checking-style access, such as checks or a debit card, and often requires a higher balance. A high-yield savings account is a savings account that pays well above the national average, usually with low or no minimum and no checks. Both are insured up to $250,000. If you mostly want the best rate on cash you rarely touch, high-yield savings often matches or beats a money market account; if you want to write checks from your savings, a money market account fits better.

Key Takeaways

  • Money market accounts and savings accounts at insured banks and credit unions are both covered by the FDIC or NCUA up to $250,000 per depositor, per institution, per ownership category.
  • Money market accounts often include check-writing and a debit card; traditional savings accounts usually do not offer check-writing (Chase, U.S. Bank).
  • U.S. Bank says high-yield savings accounts often match or exceed money market account rates, while money market accounts may use tiered rates that pay more on larger balances.
  • In November 2026 the FDIC national average rate was 0.39% for savings and 0.64% for money market accounts.
  • In April 2020 the Federal Reserve deleted the six-per-month limit on convenient transfers from savings deposits, but banks may still set their own limits.

What Is a Money Market Account?

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) defines a money market account as a type of account offered by banks and credit unions. Chase describes it as combining features of savings and checking accounts. You earn interest like a savings account, and you can usually write checks or use a debit card, though the CFPB notes that money market accounts usually limit the number of transactions by check, debit card or electronic transfer.

The CFPB says money market accounts tend to pay higher interest rates than other types of savings accounts. The trade-off is that they might require a minimum deposit, and Chase notes they may charge monthly maintenance fees, which can reduce earnings on a relatively low balance.

What Is a High-Yield Savings Account?

A high-yield savings account is a regular savings account that pays a rate well above the national average. U.S. Bank describes a savings account as a deposit account that earns interest on your balance, and notes that savings accounts often pay the same annual percentage yield (APY) regardless of balance. APY is the yearly return including compounding; see APR vs. APY.

Savings accounts usually come with low or no minimum balance but no checks or debit card. To spend the money, you usually transfer it to checking first.

Which Pays More?

It depends on the bank. On average, money market accounts pay more: the FDIC national rate was 0.64% for money market accounts and 0.39% for savings in November 2026. But averages include low-paying accounts at large banks. U.S. Bank says high-yield savings accounts often match or exceed money market rates and that neither type consistently earns more than the other. Ally similarly notes that money market accounts generally pay more than regular savings, but not always more than high-yield savings.

Money market accounts may use tiered rates, meaning a larger balance earns a higher APY. If you keep a small balance, you may land in the lowest tier. Always compare the APY you would earn at your balance.

Transaction Limits and Regulation D

For years, federal Regulation D limited savings and money market accounts to six convenient transfers or withdrawals per month. On April 24, 2020, the Federal Reserve announced an interim final rule deleting that six-per-month limit from the definition of a savings deposit. The Fed's FAQ says the rule permits banks to stop enforcing the limit but does not require them to, and that Regulation D does not require or prohibit fees for transfers above the old limit.

In practice, some banks still cap withdrawals per statement cycle or charge an excess-withdrawal fee. Read the account agreement before you open either type.

Money Market Account vs. Money Market Fund

Don't confuse a money market account with a money market fund. The CFPB says a money market mutual fund is an investment, not a savings or checking account, and it is not FDIC-insured. The CFPB notes that if the brokerage holding the fund fails, protection comes from SIPC, not the FDIC. Learn more in What Is a Brokerage Account?

Which One Should You Choose?

  • Choose high-yield savings if you want a competitive rate with no minimum, you don't need checks, and you are fine moving money to checking when you need it. This fits most emergency funds and sinking funds.
  • Choose a money market account if you want to pay large bills directly from savings by check or debit card, you can keep a balance high enough to avoid fees, and the rate at your balance is competitive.
  • Consider a CD if you won't need the money for a set period and want a fixed rate. Compare in our best CD rates guide.

Insurance limits: both account types are insured up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank or credit union, per ownership category. If you hold more than that at one bank, the excess may not be covered.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a money market account better than a high-yield savings account?

Neither is always better. Money market accounts add checks or a debit card and may pay more on large balances, while high-yield savings accounts often match or exceed their rates with lower minimums. Compare the APY you would earn at your balance.

Are money market accounts FDIC-insured?

Yes. Money market accounts at banks are FDIC-insured, and those at credit unions are NCUA-insured, up to $250,000 per depositor, per institution, per ownership category. Money market mutual funds are investments and are not FDIC-insured.

Can you write checks from a high-yield savings account?

Usually not. Traditional and high-yield savings accounts typically do not include check-writing or a debit card. Money market accounts typically do.

Do savings accounts still have a six-withdrawal limit?

Not under federal rules. The Federal Reserve deleted the six-per-month limit from Regulation D in April 2020, but banks may still set their own limits or charge excess-withdrawal fees.

What is the average money market account rate?

The FDIC national average was 0.64% for money market accounts and 0.39% for savings accounts in November 2026. High-yield accounts, by definition, pay above these averages.
