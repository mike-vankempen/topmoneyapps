How to Buy Treasury Bills (T-Bills): TreasuryDirect or a Broker

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 8 min read

General information, not investment advice. Based on IRS Publication 550, Treasury rules in 31 CFR Part 363 and FRED rate data.

You can buy Treasury bills two ways: directly from the government at TreasuryDirect.gov, starting at $100, or through a brokerage account, where you can buy at auction or on the secondary market and sell before maturity. T-bills are short-term U.S. government debt with terms from 4 to 52 weeks. You buy them at a discount and get the full face value at maturity; the difference is your interest, which is taxed federally but exempt from state and local income tax. In late November 2026, 3-month bills yielded about 4.18% on a discount basis.

Key Takeaways

  • Treasury auctions bills with terms of 4, 6, 8, 13, 17, 26 and 52 weeks; the 52-week bill is auctioned every four weeks and shorter bills weekly (Treasury auction schedule, Kiplinger).
  • T-bills are sold in amounts of $100 and multiples of $100, at a discount to face value (IRS Publication 550).
  • Interest on T-bills is subject to federal income tax but exempt from all state and local income taxes (IRS).
  • Bills bought in TreasuryDirect can't be transferred to a broker for 45 days after issue or the bill's term, whichever is less (31 CFR 363.203).
  • Secondary-market discount rates were 4.04% for 4-week bills (Nov. 23, 2026), 4.18% for 3-month bills (Nov. 24, 2026) and 4.33% for 6-month bills (Nov. 23, 2026), per FRED.

What Is a Treasury Bill?

A Treasury bill (T-bill) is a short-term loan to the U.S. government. The Bureau of the Fiscal Service says Treasury's marketable securities, including bills, are "backed by the full faith and credit of the United States government." Bills don't pay a separate interest check. Instead, IRS Publication 550 explains, they are issued at a discount, and the difference between the discounted price you pay and the face value you receive at maturity is your interest income.

Treasury's tentative auction schedule for the fourth quarter of 2026 lists bills with seven terms:

TermHow often auctioned
4, 6, 8, 13, 17 and 26 weeksWeekly
52 weeksEvery four weeks (Nov. 24 and Dec. 22, 2026 on the Q4 schedule)

In the Q4 2026 schedule, 13- and 26-week bills auction on Mondays. Bills are sold in $100 increments, so you can start small.

Discount rate vs. investment rate. Treasury quotes bills two ways. The bank discount rate is based on the face value, the amount of the discount and a 360-day year. The investment rate (also called the coupon equivalent or bond equivalent yield) is based on the purchase price and a 365- or 366-day year, so it comes out a bit higher. Use the investment rate when you compare a bill with a savings account's APY or a CD rate.

Example. At a 4.33% discount rate, a 26-week (182-day) bill with a $1,000 face value would cost about $1,000 × (1 − 0.0433 × 182 ÷ 360) = $978.11. You'd get $1,000 at maturity, so you'd earn about $21.89 in interest. This is an illustration; your price is set at auction or by the market when you buy.

Option 1: Buy T-Bills at TreasuryDirect

TreasuryDirect.gov is the Treasury's own website for buying securities with no middleman. To open an account, 31 CFR 363.11 says you need a valid Social Security number, must be 18 or older, need a U.S. address of record, and need an account at a U.S. bank or credit union that accepts electronic debits and credits.

  1. Open and fund your account. Link your bank account; the purchase price is debited from it.
  2. Choose the bill and the amount. Pick a term and enter an amount in $100 increments.
  3. Place a noncompetitive bid. You agree to accept the rate set at the auction. Under the auction rules (31 CFR 356.12), you can't bid noncompetitively for more than $10 million in one auction.
  4. Get paid at maturity. You receive the bill's full face value when it matures.

The main drawback is flexibility. Under 31 CFR 363.203, you can't transfer a bill out of TreasuryDirect for 45 calendar days after its issue date or for its full term, whichever is less. After that, you can move it to a bank or broker, which can sell it for you (31 CFR 363.208). In practice, a 4-, 6- or 8-week bill bought in TreasuryDirect will reach maturity before you could move it.

Option 2: Buy T-Bills Through a Brokerage

Most major brokerages let you buy new bills at auction or existing bills on the secondary market, where already-issued Treasuries trade. Schwab notes that marketable Treasuries can be resold in the secondary market and that their prices can fluctuate like any investment. That means you can sell before maturity, but you might get less than you paid if rates have risen.

Minimums can be higher than TreasuryDirect's $100. Fidelity lists its minimum for T-bills as one bond with a $1,000 face value. A brokerage is also convenient if you already have a brokerage account and want your bills alongside your other investments.

Auction pricing is the same either way. Schwab says all successful bidders at an auction get the same rate as the highest accepted bid, so a noncompetitive bid placed through a broker or through TreasuryDirect gets the same auction result.

How T-Bill Interest Is Taxed

IRS Publication 550 says interest from Treasury bills is subject to federal income tax but exempt from all state and local income taxes. That can make T-bills worth more after tax than a bank account with the same yield if you live in a state with an income tax; see states with no income tax.

  • When it's taxed: generally in the year the bill matures and pays you.
  • The form: you should receive Form 1099-INT showing the interest in box 3.
  • If you sell early: Fidelity notes that a bill bought at a market discount and sold before maturity may produce both interest income and a capital gain or loss.

If you want inflation protection instead of a fixed yield, compare T-bills with I bonds, which have a 12-month lockup and a $10,000 annual limit. For cash you may need any day, a high-yield savings account or money market account is simpler.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the minimum to buy a Treasury bill?

T-bills are sold in amounts of $100 and multiples of $100, so the minimum at TreasuryDirect is $100. Brokerages may set higher minimums; Fidelity lists one bond with a $1,000 face value.

Is it better to buy T-bills on TreasuryDirect or through a broker?

TreasuryDirect has a $100 minimum and you buy directly from the government at auction, but you must transfer a bill out before you can sell it. A brokerage lets you buy on the secondary market and sell before maturity; Schwab and Fidelity charge $0 for online Treasury orders as of November 2026.

Are T-bills taxed?

Interest from Treasury bills is subject to federal income tax but exempt from all state and local income taxes. You generally report it in the year the bill matures, and you should receive Form 1099-INT.

Can I sell a T-bill before it matures?

Yes, through a bank or broker on the secondary market, at the current market price. Bills bought in TreasuryDirect must first be transferred out, which you can't do for 45 days after issue or the bill's term, whichever is less.

What is the current 3-month T-bill rate?

The 3-month Treasury bill secondary market rate was 4.18% on a discount basis on November 24, 2026, according to FRED.
