AD&D Insurance vs. Life Insurance: What Each Covers

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 8 min read

General information, not insurance or legal advice. Based on CDC, BLS, OPM, state insurance regulators and insurer policy documents, checked November 2026.

Life insurance pays a death benefit for almost any cause of death, including illness, while accidental death and dismemberment (AD&D) insurance pays only when an accident causes your death or a specific listed injury, such as losing a hand or your sight. That gap matters: accidents caused about 6.4% of U.S. deaths in 2024, while heart disease and cancer caused about 42%, according to CDC data. AD&D is usually cheap and often comes free with work coverage, but it works best as an add-on, not as a replacement for life insurance.

Key Takeaways

  • AD&D insurance pays only for death or listed injuries caused by an accident; the Maryland Insurance Administration notes these policies do not pay for sickness.
  • Unintentional injuries caused 197,449 of the 3,072,666 U.S. deaths in 2024, about 6.4%, according to the CDC's National Center for Health Statistics.
  • Life insurance generally pays regardless of cause of death, subject to a two-year contestability period and, in many policies, a two-year suicide clause.
  • A typical AD&D schedule pays 100% of the benefit for death or the loss of two limbs and 50% for one hand, one foot or the sight of one eye, as in MetLife's and the federal FEGLI program's schedules.
  • In March 2026, 59% of private-industry workers and 84% of state and local government workers had access to employer life insurance, according to the BLS.

What AD&D Insurance Covers

AD&D pays a lump sum if an accident causes your death or a permanent injury that your policy specifically lists. Healthinsurance.org says policies typically cover death, loss of vision, hearing or speech, loss of a limb, or paralysis. It does not cover illness or injuries that aren't on the list. A broken arm that is expected to heal, for example, would not pay.

State regulators describe it the same way. The Maryland Insurance Administration says accident-only and AD&D policies provide benefits only for loss due to an injury and "do not pay you due to sickness."

The "dismemberment" part pays a percentage of your full benefit (often called the principal sum). Here is how two real schedules compare:

Schedules differ by insurer. Duke University's accident plan, for example, pays 75% for paraplegia, compared with 50% under The Standard's policy. A MetLife plan summary caps the total for all losses from one accident at 100% of the full amount. Always read your own policy's schedule.

Timing matters too. Under the federal employee program (FEGLI), the death or loss must happen within one year of the accident. The Standard's group policy uses 365 days, and requires paralysis to start within 180 days.

What AD&D Does Not Cover

AD&D exclusions are long, and they rule out many deaths people think of as accidental. The Office of Personnel Management (OPM) says FEGLI pays no AD&D benefit if the death or loss results from, or is contributed to by:

  • Physical or mental illness, including a heart attack or stroke. The Standard's policy lists heart attack or stroke as a separate exclusion.
  • Suicide or attempted suicide, or injuring yourself on purpose.
  • Illegal or illegally obtained drugs you take yourself. MetLife's exclusions also cover drugs or medication not taken as prescribed.
  • Driving while intoxicated, as defined by the law where you were driving.
  • War, declared or undeclared, or any act of war.

MetLife adds exclusions for committing or attempting a felony, operating an aircraft and service in the armed forces (except the U.S. National Guard). USAA notes that many AD&D policies also restrict risky activities such as scuba diving and skydiving.

Why this matters: In 2024, the CDC counted 75,761 unintentional poisoning deaths, more than the 48,308 deaths from falls or the 41,241 motor vehicle traffic deaths. Unintentional poisonings include drug overdoses, which AD&D policies commonly exclude when they involve self-administered drugs.

What Life Insurance Covers

Aflac puts it simply: life insurance generally pays a death benefit regardless of cause of death, while AD&D pays for accidental deaths and dismemberments only. Two standard provisions limit life insurance in its first years:

  • Contestability period. The North Carolina Department of Insurance says that after a policy has been in force for two years, the insurer cannot contest its validity, except for unpaid premiums.
  • Suicide clause. If the insured dies by suicide within the first two years, the death benefit is limited to the premiums paid, according to the NC Department of Insurance. The Alaska Division of Insurance describes the same two-year clause.

Why Accidents Are a Small Share of Deaths

The CDC's National Center for Health Statistics recorded 3,072,666 deaths in the U.S. in 2024. Heart disease, cancer and unintentional injuries were the top three causes.

How Much AD&D Costs

USAA says AD&D is usually inexpensive for the coverage it offers because accidental death is unlikely. Two examples from employer plans:

  • State of Michigan voluntary AD&D (MetLife), as listed in November 2026: $0.19 every two weeks for $15,000 of coverage, $3.87 for $300,000, and $12.92 for $1,000,000.
  • Federal employees (FEGLI): AD&D comes with Basic insurance and Option A at no additional charge, and is paid in addition to the regular death benefit. Option A's accidental death benefit is $10,000. AD&D cannot be carried into retirement.

At many employers, basic AD&D is employer-paid. MetLife says basic AD&D provides an accidental death benefit equal to your basic term life amount, so an accidental death can pay twice. See life insurance through work for how group coverage works and its limits.

Accidental Death Riders

You can also add accidental death coverage to an individual life policy. The Alaska Division of Insurance says an accidental death benefit rider doubles or even triples the death benefit if you die from an accident, for an extra premium. It also advises weighing whether you need it, because how much money your family needs doesn't depend on how you die.

Which One Do You Need?

  • If people depend on your income, life insurance is the coverage that pays whatever the cause of death. AD&D alone leaves out most causes of death.
  • If AD&D is free or very cheap at work, keeping it costs little. Treat it as a bonus on top of life insurance.
  • Check your beneficiaries on every policy, including coverage through work; see how to choose a beneficiary.

On taxes, the IRS says life insurance proceeds you receive as a beneficiary because of the insured person's death generally aren't included in gross income, though interest on them is taxable. See is life insurance taxable for details, and how to file a life insurance claim for the payout process.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between AD&D and life insurance?

Life insurance generally pays a death benefit no matter the cause of death, including illness. AD&D pays only if an accident causes your death or a specific injury listed in the policy, such as losing a limb or your sight, and it excludes illness.

Does AD&D cover a heart attack or stroke?

Generally no. AD&D policies exclude losses caused by illness, and some, such as The Standard's group policy, list heart attack and stroke as specific exclusions.

Is AD&D insurance worth it?

It can be worth keeping when it is free or inexpensive, such as through work, but it is not a substitute for life insurance. Accidents caused about 6.4% of U.S. deaths in 2024, so AD&D would not pay in most deaths.

How much does AD&D pay for losing a limb?

It depends on the policy schedule. A common schedule pays 50% of the full benefit for losing one hand, one foot or the sight of one eye, and 100% for losing two or more.

Does AD&D pay in addition to life insurance?

It can. Federal FEGLI pays AD&D in addition to the regular death benefit, and MetLife's basic AD&D provides an accidental death benefit equal to the basic term life amount, on top of the life benefit. Check your own policy's terms.
