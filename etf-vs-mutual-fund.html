ETF vs. Mutual Fund: Key Differences and Which to Choose

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 7 min read

General information, not investment advice. Based on SEC investor guidance at Investor.gov and FINRA.

The main difference between an ETF and a mutual fund is how you buy and sell them. ETF shares trade on a stock exchange throughout the day at market prices, while mutual fund shares are bought from and sold back to the fund at a price set once a day, after the market closes. Both pool many investors' money, both can be passive (index) or actively managed, and both charge annual expenses. ETFs also tend to pay out fewer taxable capital gains, which matters in a regular brokerage account but not in an IRA or 401(k).

Key Takeaways

  • Mutual fund shares are bought from and redeemed by the fund at net asset value (NAV), which is typically calculated at the end of each business day (SEC Investor.gov).
  • ETF shares trade on a stock exchange throughout the day at market prices, which can be above or below the fund's NAV (SEC Investor.gov, FINRA).
  • Both ETFs and mutual funds can be passively or actively managed, and both charge annual operating expenses, also called expense ratios (FINRA).
  • ETFs typically have fewer capital gains distributions than mutual funds because many use in-kind exchanges; in tax-advantaged accounts there is no tax difference (SEC Investor.gov).
  • iShares, citing Morningstar, reports that ETFs paid about 1% of the fund industry's capital gains distributions in 2025 while holding 30% of U.S. managed fund assets.

How Each One Is Bought and Priced

Mutual funds are required by law to price their shares at NAV each business day, and they typically do this after the major U.S. exchanges close, according to FINRA. When you place an order during the day, you will not know the exact price until the end of the day. You can buy shares directly from the fund company or through a broker.

ETFs also calculate a NAV each day, but you do not trade at it. You buy and sell ETF shares on an exchange, like a stock, at whatever the market price is. FINRA calls this "intraday liquidity." The SEC notes that an ETF's market price generally stays close to its NAV but can be higher (a premium) or lower (a discount), and can vary significantly at times.

Why does the ETF price stay close to NAV? Large broker-dealers called authorized participants can create or redeem ETF shares directly with the fund in big blocks (the SEC's example is 50,000 shares), usually in exchange for a basket of the underlying securities. If the ETF price drifts away from NAV, they have a profit motive to trade it back in line. Individual investors cannot redeem ETF shares with the fund; they sell on the exchange.

Fees: Expense Ratios, Commissions and Spreads

Both kinds of funds charge annual operating expenses, which FINRA notes are also called expense ratios. Beyond that, the costs show up in different places:

  • Mutual funds may charge fees directly when you buy, sell or exchange shares, and some charge account fees. Many offer several share classes, each with its own fee and expense structure.
  • ETFs generally do not charge you a fee to buy or sell, but your broker may charge a commission. You also pay the bid-ask spread: the gap between the highest price a buyer will pay and the lowest price a seller will accept. The SEC describes the spread as a hidden cost and notes that ETF sponsors publish median spreads on their websites.

The SEC says ETFs have tended to be less expensive to operate than mutual funds, but that is a tendency, not a rule. Compare the actual expense ratio of the specific funds you are considering. An index mutual fund can cost less than an actively managed ETF.

Taxes: Why ETFs Are Often More Tax-Efficient

When a mutual fund manager sells holdings at a gain, the fund may pass those capital gains to shareholders, and you owe tax on them in a taxable account even if you did not sell any shares. FINRA notes this can happen when the manager sells holdings with embedded gains.

ETFs typically have fewer capital gains distributions, and therefore lower taxes, than mutual funds, because many ETFs move securities in and out through in-kind exchanges with authorized participants instead of selling them, the SEC explains. iShares, citing Morningstar data as of December 31, 2025, reports:

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the main difference between an ETF and a mutual fund?

ETF shares trade on a stock exchange throughout the day at market prices, while mutual fund shares are bought from and sold back to the fund at net asset value, which is typically calculated once a day after the market closes.

Are ETFs cheaper than mutual funds?

Often, but not always. The SEC says ETFs have tended to be less expensive to operate than mutual funds, but you may pay a brokerage commission and the bid-ask spread. Compare the expense ratios of the specific funds.

Why are ETFs more tax-efficient than mutual funds?

Many ETFs use in-kind exchanges with authorized participants instead of selling securities, so they typically have fewer capital gains distributions. In an IRA or 401(k) there is no tax difference.

Can an ETF trade at a different price than its NAV?

Yes. An ETF's market price can be above (a premium) or below (a discount) its net asset value. It generally stays close to NAV, but the SEC notes it may vary significantly.

Are index funds ETFs or mutual funds?

Either. Both ETFs and mutual funds can be passively managed index funds or actively managed funds.
