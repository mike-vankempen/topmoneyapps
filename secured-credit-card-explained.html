How Secured Credit Cards Work: Deposits, Limits and Upgrades

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 8 min read

General information, not personalized advice. Based on the CFPB, FTC, Philadelphia Fed and card issuers' own pages, checked November 2026.

A secured credit card is a real credit card backed by a refundable cash deposit, which usually equals your credit limit. You buy things on the card, get a monthly bill and pay it like any other card, and the issuer reports your payments to the credit bureaus, which is how it builds your credit history. The deposit is held as collateral: you can't use it to pay your bill, and you get it back when you upgrade to a regular card or close the account with the balance paid. As of November 2026, minimum deposits at major issuers run from $49 to $200.

Key Takeaways

  • A secured credit card requires a cash deposit that is held as collateral and typically equals your credit limit; it is refundable when you close the account in good standing or upgrade.
  • As of November 2026, Capital One Platinum Secured and Discover it Secured accept refundable deposits of $49, $99 or $200 depending on creditworthiness, with credit lines starting at $200.
  • Secured cards often carry high interest rates: the Philadelphia Fed found the share of new secured cards with an APR of at least 25% rose from 2% to 80% between 2015 and 2022.
  • Payment history is the largest part of a FICO Score at 35%, followed by amounts owed at 30%, so paying a secured card on time and keeping the balance low matter most.
  • Prepaid and debit cards generally do not build credit history, according to the FTC and CFPB, while many secured card issuers report your use to the credit bureaus.

How a Secured Credit Card Works

  1. You put down a deposit. The FTC says a secured card requires you to deposit money with the bank or card issuer, usually a percentage of your credit limit, and sometimes up to 100%. Experian says the deposit acts as collateral and is typically equal to your credit limit.
  2. You use it like a normal credit card. You make purchases up to your limit, get a monthly statement and make at least the minimum payment. The CFPB explains that when you pay the bill, you restore your available spending back to the amount you put in.
  3. The deposit is not your payment. Experian notes you can't use the security deposit to pay your monthly bill. If you carry a balance, you pay interest like on any card.
  4. The issuer reports to the credit bureaus. The FTC says many secured card issuers report how you use your card to the credit reporting agencies. Capital One says your activity needs to be reported to at least one of the three major bureaus to build credit.
  5. You get the deposit back. You get it back when you upgrade to an unsecured card or close the account with the balance paid in full (details below).

Secured Card vs. Debit and Prepaid Cards

The CFPB puts it plainly: when you use a debit card or cash, you are not borrowing money you need to repay later. That's why those cards don't create the payment record a credit score is built on.

What the Data Says About Secured Cards

A May 2024 Philadelphia Fed report on the secured card market found:

  • Limits are small. About half of secured card borrowers had $200 limits, and the average limit fell from $482 in 2013 to $362 in 2022.
  • APRs rose sharply. The share of new secured cards with an APR of at least 25% went from 2% in 2015 to 80% in 2022.
  • Annual fees are rare. Only about 4% of secured cards opened in 2022 had an annual fee.
  • Many people graduate. Almost 33% of borrowers who had no credit score when they opened the card had graduated to an unsecured card by month 12, and about 50% by month 30.

How to Get Your Deposit Back

There are two ways:

  • Upgrade to an unsecured card. Capital One says it periodically reviews whether you're eligible to upgrade, and returns your deposit as a statement credit when you do. Citi starts upgrade reviews at 9 months and then every 12 months. Bank of America says it periodically reviews accounts and you may qualify to have your deposit returned, but not all customers will. Discover's FAQ describes automatic monthly reviews starting at 7 months, but NerdWallet reported in May 2026 that Discover will stop offering those automatic reviews, after Capital One's 2025 acquisition of Discover, so check current terms.
  • Close the account with a $0 balance. Capital One says that if your account is closed, your deposit is applied to your balance within 7 to 10 days and any remaining amount is refunded by check after two billing cycles.

How to Build Credit With a Secured Card

FICO says payment history makes up 35% of a FICO Score, amounts owed 30%, length of credit history 15%, new credit 10% and credit mix 10%. To get a FICO Score at all, you need at least one account open for six months or more, and at least one account reported to the bureau within the past six months.

  1. Pay on time, every month. Set up autopay for at least the minimum. The CFPB notes that negative information like late payments can stay on your report for seven years.
  2. Keep the balance low. On a $200 limit, even a $100 balance uses half your available credit. Our guide to credit utilization explains why this matters.
  3. Pay in full to avoid interest. With APRs near 30%, carrying a balance gets expensive fast.
  4. Check your credit reports. Confirm the card shows up and is reported correctly. If not, see how to dispute a credit report error.

Fees and Risks to Watch

The FTC says secured cards generally, but not always, have higher APRs and higher annual fees than unsecured cards, and that money spent on fees reduces your available credit. Federal rules limit this: under Regulation Z, fees charged to the card in the first year generally can't exceed 25% of the credit limit when the account is opened. Late, over-the-limit and returned-payment fees don't count toward that cap. The CFPB's official commentary says a security deposit charged to the card account counts as a fee under this rule.

The CFPB says fees and interest rates can be high for secured cards, but using one can help you establish a credit record. Compare the APR and fees before you apply.

Alternatives to a Secured Card

  • Credit-builder loan. The CFPB says you build credit and savings at the same time: the loan money is held in a locked account while you make payments, often over 6 to 24 months, and the payments are reported to the credit bureaus.
  • Authorized user. You're added to someone else's card. The CFPB notes the primary cardholder is legally responsible for the charges, and not all issuers report authorized users' accounts to the bureaus.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do secured credit cards build credit?

Yes, if the issuer reports your account to the credit bureaus. Many secured card issuers report to all three bureaus. Paying on time and keeping your balance low build your history, because payment history is 35% of a FICO Score.

Do you get your secured card deposit back?

Yes. The deposit is refundable. You typically get it back when the issuer upgrades you to an unsecured card or when you close the account and pay the balance in full.

Can I use my security deposit to pay my bill?

No. The deposit is collateral for the issuer. You still need to pay your monthly bill from your own funds, and interest applies to any balance you carry.

How long does it take to build credit with a secured card?

To get a FICO Score you need at least one account open for six months or more and reported to a bureau in the past six months. Upgrade reviews vary by issuer; Citi, for example, starts reviews at 9 months.

What is the minimum deposit for a secured credit card?

It depends on the card. As of November 2026, Capital One Platinum Secured and Discover it Secured accept $49, $99 or $200 based on creditworthiness, while Bank of America and Citi require at least $200.
