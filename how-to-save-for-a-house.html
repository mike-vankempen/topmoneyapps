How to Save for a Down Payment on a House

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 9 min read

General information, not financial advice. Data from the National Association of Realtors, the CFPB and HUD.

To save for a down payment, set a target based on the loan you will use (3% to 20% of the price, plus 2% to 5% for closing costs), divide it by your timeline to get a monthly amount, and automate that amount into an FDIC-insured savings account. First-time buyers put down a median 9% in 2026, according to the National Association of Realtors (NAR). On the October 2026 median existing-home price of $416,700, that is about $37,500. Here is the step-by-step plan.

Key Takeaways

  • First-time home buyers made a median down payment of 9% and repeat buyers 19%, according to NAR's 2026 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers.
  • The median existing-home sales price was $416,700 in October 2026, according to NAR, so a 9% down payment on that price is about $37,500 and 20% is $83,340.
  • FHA loans allow down payments as low as 3.5%, Fannie Mae HomeReady and Freddie Mac Home Possible allow 3%, and VA and USDA guaranteed loans can require no down payment.
  • Closing costs typically range from 2% to 5% of the purchase price, according to the CFPB, on top of the down payment.
  • First-time buyers can withdraw up to $10,000 from an IRA without the 10% early-withdrawal additional tax, according to IRS Topic 557.

Step 1: Set Your Down Payment Target

Your target is the home price you expect multiplied by the down payment percentage your loan requires, plus closing costs. Many buyers put down far less than 20%. NAR's 2026 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers found a median down payment of 9% for first-time buyers and 19% for repeat buyers. The median first-time buyer was 40 years old, matching the record high.

Here is what common down payment levels look like on NAR's October 2026 median existing-home price of $416,700. Use your own local price for a real plan.

Step 2: Know the Minimum Down Payment for Each Loan

  • FHA loans: HUD says your down payment can be as low as 3.5% of the purchase price. Under HUD's credit score rules, borrowers with a score of 580 or higher qualify for maximum financing, while scores from 500 to 579 are limited to 90% loan-to-value, which means 10% down. The CFPB notes that mortgage insurance is required for all FHA loans.
  • Conventional 3% programs: Fannie Mae's HomeReady and Freddie Mac's Home Possible allow down payments as low as 3%. Both cap income at 80% of the area median income.
  • VA loans: eligible borrowers can often buy with no down payment if the sales price is not higher than the appraised value, and VA loans do not require private mortgage insurance. A one-time funding fee may apply.
  • USDA guaranteed loans: offer 100% financing in eligible rural areas for households with income no higher than 115% of the area median.

Step 3: Decide Whether to Aim for 20%

The CFPB says that if your down payment is less than 20% of the price, you likely need to pay for mortgage insurance. On a conventional loan, private mortgage insurance (PMI) is not permanent. According to the OCC's HelpWithMyBank.gov, you can ask your servicer to cancel PMI when your loan balance falls below 80% of the home's original value, and it must be cancelled automatically once the loan-to-value ratio reaches 78% based on the original payment schedule.

So the trade-off is time versus cost: waiting to save 20% avoids PMI, while buying sooner with 3% to 10% down adds a monthly insurance cost until you reach the cancellation point. Neither is right for everyone. Compare the monthly payment in both cases, and see our rent vs. buy analysis for the cost of renting while you save.

Step 4: Turn the Target Into a Monthly Number

Divide your total goal (down payment plus closing costs, minus what you already have) by the number of months until you want to buy. For example, saving $37,503, the 9% down payment above, takes about $781 a month for four years or $625 a month for five years, before any interest. If the monthly number is too high, you have three levers: a longer timeline, a lower price target, or a loan with a smaller down payment.

  1. Automate it. Set a transfer to a separate savings account on payday so the money moves before you can spend it.
  2. Keep your emergency fund separate. A down payment fund that also pays for car repairs rarely reaches its goal. Size your safety net with our emergency fund calculator.
  3. Direct windfalls to the fund. Tax refunds, bonuses and raises can shorten the timeline without changing your monthly budget.
  4. Track progress. A budgeting app can show the goal next to your spending.

Step 5: Keep the Money Safe and Earning Interest

Money you plan to spend in a few years needs to be there when you find a home. FDIC-insured savings accounts, money market accounts and CDs keep your principal protected up to insurance limits. Rates vary widely between banks. The FDIC's national average was 0.39% for savings and 0.64% for money market accounts in November 2026 (FDIC data via FRED). Rates vary widely between banks, so compare current rates in our best high-yield savings accounts guide and our best CD rates page. A CD can fit if you know your purchase date.

Step 6: Use Gifts and Down Payment Assistance

You do not have to fund it all yourself. In NAR's 2026 Profile, first-time buyers drew on these sources for their down payment (buyers could name more than one):

Gifts: lenders need paperwork. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide requires a gift letter that states the amount, says no repayment is expected, and lists the donor's name, address, phone number and relationship to you. For a one-unit principal residence, Fannie Mae does not require a minimum contribution from your own funds. Gifts and grants are also allowed under Freddie Mac's Home Possible.

Assistance programs: the CFPB says you could qualify for a down payment assistance program through your state or local government or a nonprofit, and suggests talking to a local housing counselor. HUD also points buyers to state and local programs.

Step 7: Know the Rules Before Using Retirement Money

Tapping retirement savings is possible but has costs, because that money stops growing for retirement.

  • IRAs: IRS Topic 557 lists an exception to the 10% additional tax on early distributions for up to $10,000 used in a qualified first-time home purchase. The exception applies to IRAs, not to 401(k) plans, and it removes the additional tax only; ordinary income tax rules for the distribution still apply.
  • 401(k) loans: if your plan allows loans, the IRS limit is generally the lesser of $50,000 or the greater of $10,000 or 50% of your vested balance. Loans normally must be repaid within five years, but a loan used to buy your principal residence can have a longer term.

Lenders also review your debts and credit when you apply. See what lenders look for in our guides to a good debt-to-income ratio and a good credit score.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much should I save for a down payment?

It depends on your loan. Minimums range from 0% for eligible VA and USDA loans to 3% for HomeReady and Home Possible and 3.5% for FHA. First-time buyers put down a median 9% in 2026, according to NAR. Budget another 2% to 5% of the price for closing costs.

Do you need 20% down to buy a house?

No. Many loans allow 3% to 3.5% down, and some allow none. With less than 20% down on a conventional loan you will likely pay mortgage insurance, which can be cancelled once your balance falls below 80% of the home's original value.

Where should I keep my down payment savings?

Most buyers use FDIC-insured savings, money market accounts or CDs so the money is safe when they need it. The FDIC national average was 0.39% for savings and 0.64% for money market accounts in November 2026, so it pays to compare banks.

Can I use my IRA for a down payment?

Yes. First-time buyers can withdraw up to $10,000 from an IRA without the 10% additional tax on early distributions, according to the IRS. Regular income tax rules still apply, and the exception does not cover 401(k) plans.

Can family help with a down payment?

Yes. Lenders generally accept gifts with a signed gift letter stating the amount and that no repayment is expected. In NAR's 2026 Profile, 25% of first-time buyers used gifts or loans from family and friends.
