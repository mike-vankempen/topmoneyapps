401(k) vs. Roth IRA: Which Should You Fund First?

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 8 min read

General information, not tax or investment advice. Confirm details with the IRS and your plan or account provider.

You do not have to choose: you can contribute to a 401(k) and a Roth IRA in the same year, because each has its own limit. For 2026 the 401(k) employee limit is $24,500 and the IRA limit is $7,500. A common order is to put enough into the 401(k) to get any employer match, then fund a Roth IRA, then go back to the 401(k). Here is how the two accounts compare and why that order is popular.

Key Takeaways

  • For 2026 the 401(k) employee deferral limit is $24,500, and workers 50 and older can add an $8,000 catch-up, or $11,250 at ages 60 to 63 (IRS).
  • The 2026 IRA limit is $7,500, plus a $1,100 catch-up at 50 or older, and it is separate from the 401(k) limit.
  • Roth IRA contributions phase out at modified AGI of $153,000 to $168,000 for single filers and $242,000 to $252,000 for joint filers; Roth 401(k) contributions have no income limit.
  • Neither Roth IRAs nor designated Roth 401(k) accounts require minimum distributions while the owner is alive; traditional 401(k)s generally require them from age 73.
  • Final IRS rules require catch-up contributions by certain higher-income workers to be Roth, generally starting in taxable years beginning after December 31, 2026.

401(k) vs. Roth IRA: Side by Side (2026)

A 401(k) is a retirement plan offered by an employer. Some plans also offer a Roth option, which the IRS calls a designated Roth account. A Roth IRA is an individual account you open yourself at a brokerage or bank.

Contribution Limits for 2026

The 401(k) limit is more than three times the IRA limit. For 2026 you can defer up to $24,500 of your pay into a 401(k). If you are 50 or older, you can add a catch-up contribution of $8,000, for a total of $32,500. If you are 60, 61, 62 or 63, a higher catch-up of $11,250 applies instead, for a total of $35,750. The same employee limit applies to 403(b) plans, governmental 457 plans and the federal Thrift Savings Plan.

The IRA limit is $7,500, or $8,600 if you are 50 or older. It covers traditional and Roth IRAs combined, and it is a separate bucket from your 401(k). Someone under 50 can put $24,500 into a 401(k) and $7,500 into a Roth IRA, $32,000 in total, before counting any employer money.

Income Limits: Where the Roth IRA Gets Restricted

A Roth IRA has income limits; a 401(k) does not. For 2026, your ability to contribute to a Roth IRA phases out between $153,000 and $168,000 of modified AGI if you are single or head of household, and between $242,000 and $252,000 if you are married filing jointly. A Roth 401(k) has no such limit, according to the IRS, so higher earners whose plans offer a Roth option can still make Roth contributions at work.

New for higher earners: under final IRS regulations (IR-2025-91), catch-up contributions made by certain higher-income participants must be designated as Roth contributions. The rule generally applies to contributions in taxable years beginning after December 31, 2026, with later dates for some governmental and collectively bargained plans. Ask your plan administrator whether it applies to you.

Why a Common Order Is Match, Then Roth IRA, Then 401(k)

Many savers fund the two accounts in this order. It is a general framework, not advice for your situation:

  1. Contribute enough to your 401(k) to get the full employer match. A match is money your employer adds on top of your pay, and you only get it by contributing. Check your plan documents for the match formula and any vesting schedule.
  2. Fund a Roth IRA, if you are eligible. A Roth IRA lets you choose your own provider and investments, and you can withdraw your contributions at any time without tax or penalty.
  3. Go back to the 401(k). After the Roth IRA is full, keep adding to the 401(k) up to the $24,500 limit if you can afford it.

The order can change. If your 401(k) has low-cost funds and no match, or if your income is above the Roth IRA phase-out range, the 401(k) alone may be simpler. If you want a lower tax bill this year, traditional 401(k) contributions reduce your taxable pay now; see Roth vs. traditional for how that trade-off works.

Access to Your Money

The Roth IRA is more flexible before retirement. Fidelity and Schwab both note that you can withdraw your Roth IRA contributions at any time, tax- and penalty-free. Earnings are different: to take them out tax-free you generally need to be 59½ and meet the 5-year rule.

A 401(k) is more restrictive. The IRS says withdrawals from a designated Roth account are allowed only when the plan's terms permit. On the other hand, a 401(k) can offer loans if the plan allows them; a Roth IRA cannot. Taxable distributions taken before age 59½ may face a 10% additional tax unless an exception applies.

Required Minimum Distributions

Traditional 401(k) balances are subject to required minimum distributions, generally starting at age 73. The IRS says RMD rules do not apply to Roth IRAs or designated Roth accounts in a 401(k) or 403(b) plan while the owner is alive. Beneficiaries who inherit either account are still subject to RMD rules. More detail is in our 2026 RMD guide.

What Happens When You Leave Your Job

A Roth IRA stays with you no matter where you work. A 401(k) stays with your old employer's plan unless you move it. You can usually roll it into your new employer's plan or into an IRA; our guide on how to roll over a 401(k) walks through the steps and the tax traps.

Bottom Line

A 401(k) gives you a much higher limit, possible employer money and, for Roth 401(k)s, Roth treatment with no income limit. A Roth IRA gives you more control over investments, access to your contributions and no RMDs. Using both is common. Frequently Asked Questions

Should I contribute to a 401(k) or a Roth IRA first?

A common order is to contribute enough to your 401(k) to get any employer match, then fund a Roth IRA if you are eligible, then add more to the 401(k). This is a general framework; the right order depends on your plan's match, fees and fund choices and on your income.

Can I have a 401(k) and a Roth IRA at the same time?

Yes. The 401(k) limit and the IRA limit are separate. For 2026 someone under 50 can contribute up to $24,500 to a 401(k) and $7,500 to a Roth IRA, as long as their income allows Roth IRA contributions.

What are the 2026 401(k) and Roth IRA contribution limits?

For 2026 the 401(k) employee limit is $24,500, with an $8,000 catch-up at age 50 or older or $11,250 at ages 60 to 63. The IRA limit is $7,500, with a $1,100 catch-up at age 50 or older.

Is there an income limit for a Roth 401(k)?

No. Roth 401(k) contributions are not limited by filing status or adjusted gross income. Roth IRA contributions phase out at modified AGI of $153,000 to $168,000 for single filers and $242,000 to $252,000 for joint filers in 2026.

Do Roth 401(k)s have required minimum distributions?

No. According to the IRS, RMD rules do not apply to Roth IRAs or designated Roth accounts in a 401(k) or 403(b) plan while the owner is alive. Traditional 401(k) balances generally require distributions starting at age 73.
