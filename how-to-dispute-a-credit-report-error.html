How to Dispute a Credit Report Error

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 8 min read

General information, not legal advice. Based on the Fair Credit Reporting Act, CFPB and FTC guidance, checked November 2026.

To dispute a credit report error, get your free reports at AnnualCreditReport.com, then file a dispute with each bureau that shows the mistake (Equifax, Experian or TransUnion) and, ideally, with the company that reported it. Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA), the bureau generally must finish its investigation within 30 days and must correct or delete information that is inaccurate, incomplete or can't be verified. Disputing is free, and you don't need a paid credit repair service to do it.

Key Takeaways

  • Equifax, Experian and TransUnion have permanently extended free weekly online credit reports at AnnualCreditReport.com, according to the FTC.
  • Under the FCRA, a credit bureau must finish investigating a dispute within 30 days of receiving it, extendable by up to 15 days if you send more information during that period.
  • The bureau must forward your dispute to the company that supplied the information within 5 business days and send you written results within 5 business days after it finishes.
  • Credit or consumer reporting made up about 88% of the roughly 6.6 million complaints the CFPB received in 2025, and the most common issue was incorrect information on a report.
  • Most negative information can stay on your report for 7 years and bankruptcies for 10 years, so only inaccurate items can be removed through a dispute.

How Common Are Credit Report Errors?

In a Federal Trade Commission study of 1,001 people and 2,968 credit reports (published in 2013), about 1 in 5 participants had an error on at least one report, and 5% had errors that could have led to less favorable loan terms. Four in five people who disputed saw a change to their report. A 2024 Consumer Reports and WorkMoney project found that 44% of its 4,000-plus volunteers who checked their reports found at least one error, though that group was self-selected and not a national sample.

Complaints are rising. The CFPB's 2025 Consumer Response Annual Report counted about 6,635,400 complaints in 2025, up from 3,187,900 in 2024. About 5,806,800 (88%) were about credit or consumer reporting, and "incorrect information on your report" was the most common issue.

Step 1: Get Your Free Credit Reports

Go to AnnualCreditReport.com, the only site authorized to provide the free reports required by federal law. You can also call 1-877-322-8228. The three bureaus now offer free weekly online reports there. Check all three, because lenders don't always report to every bureau and an error may appear on only one.

Step 2: Find the Errors

The CFPB lists these as common errors worth disputing:

Type of errorExamples
Identity errorsWrong name, phone or address; accounts that belong to someone with a similar name; accounts opened by an identity thief
Account statusClosed accounts shown as open; you listed as owner when you're only an authorized user; payments wrongly marked late
Wrong datesIncorrect date of last payment, date opened or date of first delinquency
Data managementCorrected information that reappears; one account listed more than once or under different creditor names
Balance errorsWrong balance or wrong credit limit

If you see accounts you never opened, treat it as identity theft: report it at IdentityTheft.gov and consider a credit freeze at all three bureaus.

Step 3: Gather Your Documents

The CFPB and FTC suggest including your full name, address and phone number; the report's confirmation number; each error with its account number; why it is wrong; and what you want done (removed or corrected). Attach a copy of the report with the items circled and copies, not originals, of anything that supports your case, such as statements or payment confirmations. Keep copies of everything you send.

Step 4: Dispute With Each Credit Bureau

File a separate dispute with every bureau that shows the error. Online is usually fastest. If you mail it, the FTC and CFPB suggest certified mail with a return receipt so you can prove when it arrived, since the 30-day clock starts when the bureau receives your dispute.

Step 5: Dispute With the Company That Reported It

The company that sent the information to the bureau, called the "furnisher" (your lender, card issuer or a collection agency), has its own duties under the FCRA and Regulation V. You can dispute directly with it about whether you owe the debt, the account terms (such as balance or credit limit) and your payment history. Send the dispute to the address the furnisher lists on your credit report or one it names for disputes. The CFPB says furnishers generally must investigate and respond within 30 days, and if the information was wrong, they must notify every bureau they reported it to.

The CFPB notes you may need to dispute with both the bureau and the furnisher to fix an error everywhere.

If the dispute changes your report, the bureau sends you a free updated copy, and the FTC says it doesn't count as your free annual report. You can also ask the bureau to send the correction to anyone who received your report for a job in the past 2 years, or for any other purpose in the past 6 months.

What If Your Dispute Is Rejected?

  1. Add a statement of dispute. You can ask the bureau to add a brief statement to your file (it may limit you to 100 words). Future reports must note that the item is disputed.
  2. Send new evidence. A dispute the bureau calls frivolous often lacks documents. Refile with proof.
  3. File a complaint. Submit one to the CFPB at consumerfinance.gov/complaint or (855) 411-2372, or to your state attorney general.
  4. Know your legal rights. If a bureau or furnisher violates the FCRA, the CFPB says you may be able to sue in state or federal court. Time limits apply; lawhelp.org lists free legal aid.

What You Can't Dispute Away

Accurate negative information can stay on your report: most items for 7 years and bankruptcies for 10 years. No one can legally remove correct information early. Disputes are free, so be cautious of anyone charging you to do the same thing.

Medical debt in 2026: A CFPB rule that would have removed most medical debt from credit reports was vacated by a federal court in Texas on July 11, 2025. The three bureaus' voluntary policies still apply: paid medical collections were removed starting July 1, 2022, unpaid ones wait a year before appearing, and medical collections under $500 have been excluded since April 2023. If a covered item shows up, dispute it. The policy does not cover medical bills you put on a credit card.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a credit bureau have to investigate a dispute?

Generally 30 days from the day it receives your dispute. The bureau can take up to 15 more days if you send relevant information during those 30 days, and disputes filed after receiving your free annual report have 45 days. The bureau must send you the results within 5 business days after it finishes.

Does it cost money to dispute a credit report error?

No. Disputing with Equifax, Experian, TransUnion or the company that reported the information is free, whether you file online, by phone or by mail. You do not need a paid credit repair service to dispute an error.

Should I dispute online or by mail?

Online is usually fastest. If you mail a dispute, the FTC and CFPB suggest certified mail with a return receipt, copies of your documents rather than originals, and keeping copies of everything, so you can prove when the bureau received it.

What if the credit bureau says the information is accurate?

You can add a brief statement of dispute to your file, refile with new evidence, dispute directly with the company that reported it, and file a complaint with the CFPB or your state attorney general. If a bureau or furnisher broke the FCRA, you may be able to sue, but time limits apply.

Can I remove accurate negative information from my credit report?

No. Only information that is inaccurate, incomplete or can't be verified must be corrected or removed. Accurate negative items generally stay for 7 years, and bankruptcies for 10 years.
