Final Expense Life Insurance Explained: How Burial Insurance Works

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 8 min read

General information, not insurance, legal or financial advice. Based on NFDA, FTC and state insurance regulator guidance, plus insurers' own policy pages.

Final expense insurance, also called burial insurance, is a small whole life insurance policy meant to pay for your funeral and other end-of-life bills. Policies usually range from about $2,000 to $25,000, premiums stay level for life, and many require no medical exam. The main catch: guaranteed-acceptance policies typically pay only your premiums back, plus a little, if you die of natural causes in the first two years.

Key Takeaways

  • Final expense insurance is a small whole life policy, usually sold to people 45 and older, with death benefits that typically top out around $25,000.
  • The national median cost of a funeral with viewing and burial was $8,300 in 2023, and $6,280 for a funeral with cremation, according to the National Funeral Directors Association.
  • Guaranteed issue policies from Mutual of Omaha and USAA accept applicants aged 45 to 85 with no health questions, but pay only premiums plus 10% for a death from natural causes in the first two years.
  • On Mutual of Omaha's Guaranteed Whole Life rate chart (checked November 2026), $10,000 of coverage costs a 70-year-old man $86.70 a month, so total premiums pass $10,000 in under 10 years.
  • A final expense policy pays the beneficiary you choose; a preneed funeral contract is tied to a specific funeral home, and state rules on prepaid funds vary.

What Final Expense Insurance Is

Final expense insurance is permanent life insurance with a small death benefit. NerdWallet describes "burial insurance" as a marketing term rather than a distinct policy type: it is usually a whole life policy, open to people 45 and older, with coverage that typically starts at $1,000 and maxes out at $25,000. Whole life means the coverage lasts as long as you keep paying premiums, and the policy builds a small cash value over time.

The money goes to the beneficiary you name, not to a funeral home. Your beneficiary can use it for a funeral, cremation, a headstone, medical bills, or anything else. Mutual of Omaha's policy disclosure makes the same point: its guaranteed policy "does not specifically cover funeral goods or services and may not cover the entire cost of your funeral." Simplified Issue vs. Guaranteed Issue

Most final expense policies skip the medical exam. They differ in how much the insurer asks about your health:

FeatureSimplified issueGuaranteed issue
Medical examNoNo
Health questionsYes, a questionnaire; you can be declinedNone; acceptance is guaranteed within the age range
Records checkedOften the MIB file and prescription drug history (NerdWallet)No health review
Typical maximumSimplified whole life often caps at $25,000 to $50,000 (NerdWallet)Usually up to $25,000 (Mutual of Omaha)
PriceCosts more than a fully underwritten policyUsually higher still, because there is no underwriting
Waiting periodDepends on the policy and your answersCommonly a two-year graded benefit

Underwriting is the insurer's review of your risk. With simplified issue, you answer health and lifestyle questions, and the insurer may also check the MIB (a database showing past life insurance applications) and your prescription records. If you are in reasonable health, simplified issue usually costs less than guaranteed issue for the same amount.

Guaranteed issue is the fallback when health problems would get you declined elsewhere. Examples of guaranteed policies, as described on the insurers' own pages in November 2026:

  • Mutual of Omaha Guaranteed Whole Life: ages 45 to 85 (50 to 75 in New York), $2,000 to $25,000 of coverage, no health questions.
  • USAA Guaranteed Whole Life: ages 45 to 85 (50 to 75 in New York), fixed amounts from $2,000 to $25,000, no health questions.
  • State Farm Guaranteed Issue Final Expense: $10,000 to $15,000 of coverage, no medical questions or exams; not available in New York.

Graded Benefits and Waiting Periods

A graded death benefit means the policy pays less than the full face amount if you die early in the policy. On guaranteed issue policies, the graded period is usually the first two years:

  • Mutual of Omaha and USAA: a death from natural causes in the first two years pays all premiums paid plus 10%. Accidental death pays the full benefit from the start. After two years, the full benefit is paid for any cause.
  • State Farm: a death from natural causes in the first two years returns the premiums paid plus interest at the rate stated in the contract.

NerdWallet notes that some guaranteed issue policies have waiting periods of two to three years, so read this section of any policy before you buy. If you are likely to qualify for simplified issue, a policy that pays the full benefit from day one may be worth the health questionnaire.

Watch the math: a graded policy bought by someone in poor health may pay out only the premiums plus 10% if death comes within two years. That protects against losing money, but it is not the same as having $10,000 or $25,000 of coverage.

How Much Coverage and What It Costs

Start with what a funeral costs where you live. The National Funeral Directors Association's (NFDA) 2023 General Price List Study put the national median cost of a funeral with viewing and burial at $8,300, up from $7,848 two years earlier. The median for a funeral with cremation was $6,280, up from $5,810. The FTC notes that immediate burial and direct cremation are usually the least expensive arrangements.

Premiums depend on your age at purchase, your sex and the coverage amount. Here are sample monthly premiums for $10,000 of guaranteed issue coverage from Mutual of Omaha's national rate chart, checked November 2026:

These are guaranteed issue rates, which carry no health review. Simplified issue rates for a healthy applicant are often lower. For a sense of how term and whole life prices rise with age more generally, see average life insurance cost by age.

The Alaska Division of Insurance warns buyers to make sure "the amount paid in will not quickly exceed the possible benefit." Using the chart above, a 70-year-old man paying $86.70 a month has paid more than $10,000 in premiums after 116 months, just under 10 years. An 80-year-old man paying $157.10 a month passes $10,000 in 64 months. If you live longer than that, you will have paid more than your family receives.

Final Expense Insurance vs. Term Life, Savings and Preneed Plans

OptionHow it worksMain trade-off
Final expense (whole life)Small permanent policy; level premiums; pays your beneficiaryHigh cost per dollar of coverage; graded benefit on guaranteed issue
Term lifeCovers a set number of years; usually requires underwritingOften cheaper if you are healthy, but coverage can end before you die
Savings accountYou set money aside yourselfNo death benefit beyond what you have saved; full access while alive
Preneed funeral contractAgreement with a funeral home for specific goods and services, funded by a trust or an insurance policyTied to one provider; refund and transfer rules vary by state

Term life. NerdWallet says term coverage could be cheaper if you are in your 50s or 60s, healthy and willing to take a medical exam. The trade-off is that term coverage ends after a set period.

Savings. The Alaska Division of Insurance says a savings account may be the better choice in some cases. Money in a high-yield savings account stays yours and earns interest, and you never pay in more than you keep.

Preneed contracts. The FTC says state laws govern prepaid funeral arrangements and that protections vary. Some states require part of the payment to go into a trust; others require the funeral home to buy a life insurance policy with the death benefit assigned to the funeral home. The Texas Department of Insurance warns that funeral insurance "can be expensive compared to other types of life insurance." Alaska's regulator adds that if a policy does not guarantee funeral prices, you should name a personal beneficiary rather than the funeral home.

Who Final Expense Insurance Fits

The Alaska Division of Insurance lists the people who may consider burial insurance: those without life insurance, those who cannot get term life coverage, those with limited savings, and those who want a policy set aside only for final expenses. It is usually a poor fit if you already have enough savings or life insurance to cover a funeral, or if you are healthy enough to buy more coverage for less through a fully underwritten policy.

How to Compare Policies and Avoid Pitfalls

  • Try simplified issue first. If you can answer the health questions, you may get a lower price and a full benefit from day one.
  • Read the graded benefit section. Check how long it lasts and what it pays: premiums back, premiums plus 10%, or premiums plus interest.
  • Compare the same face amount. Get quotes for the same coverage at two or three insurers and add up the yearly cost.
  • Do the break-even math. Divide the death benefit by the annual premium to see how many years until you have paid in more than the policy pays out.
  • Name the right beneficiary. Our beneficiary guide covers how to name and update one.
  • Use the free look period. Texas policies, for example, have a free look period of at least 10 to 20 days in which you can cancel for a full refund.
  • Check the seller. Alaska's regulator suggests checking the company with the Better Business Bureau before you buy, because scams and unexpected expenses occur.

When the time comes, the FTC's Funeral Rule helps your family shop. Funeral homes must give price information over the phone if asked, and must give you a General Price List to keep that lists 16 specific items and services. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between final expense insurance and burial insurance?

In most cases there is none. Both names describe a small whole life policy meant to cover a funeral and other end-of-life bills. The death benefit goes to the beneficiary you name, who can spend it on anything.

How much does final expense insurance cost?

It depends on your age, sex, health and the amount. On Mutual of Omaha's Guaranteed Whole Life rate chart (checked November 2026), $10,000 of coverage costs $40.20 a month for a 50-year-old man and $86.70 a month for a 70-year-old man.

Does final expense insurance pay out right away?

Not always. Guaranteed issue policies usually have a two-year graded period. USAA and Mutual of Omaha, for example, pay premiums plus 10% for a death from natural causes in the first two years, and the full amount for accidental death from day one.

How much final expense coverage do I need?

Start with local funeral prices. The NFDA's national median for a funeral with viewing and burial was $8,300 in 2023, and $6,280 for a funeral with cremation. Add any other bills your family would have to pay.

Is a preneed funeral contract the same as final expense insurance?

No. A preneed contract is an agreement with a funeral home for specific goods and services, and any insurance behind it is usually assigned to the funeral home. A final expense policy pays your own beneficiary.
