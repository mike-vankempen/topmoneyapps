What Is a 529 Plan? How It Works, Rules and 2026 Changes

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 8 min read

General information, not tax advice. Based on IRS Topic 313 and the MSRB 529 investor guide.

A 529 plan is a state-sponsored account for education savings. Your contributions are not deductible on your federal return, but earnings grow tax-free and withdrawals are tax-free when used for qualified education expenses, from college to K-12 tuition and, since 2025, some job-credential programs. For 2026 the federal limit on K-12 withdrawals doubled to $20,000 per year. Below: how 529 plans work, what they cover and what to watch.

Key Takeaways

  • A 529 plan, which the IRS calls a qualified tuition program, is set up by a state or state agency to prepay or save for a beneficiary's education expenses.
  • Earnings in a 529 plan grow tax-free and withdrawals are not taxed when used for qualified education expenses; contributions are not deductible on your federal return.
  • Starting in 2026, up to $20,000 per year per beneficiary can be withdrawn for K-12 expenses, up from $10,000, according to the IRS.
  • Unused 529 money can be rolled into the beneficiary's Roth IRA, up to a $35,000 lifetime limit, if the 529 account has been open at least 15 years.
  • The earnings portion of a non-qualified withdrawal is subject to income tax plus an additional 10% federal tax.

How a 529 Plan Works

The IRS defines a qualified tuition program (QTP), also called a section 529 plan, as a program set up by a state or a state agency or instrumentality that lets you either prepay a beneficiary's qualified higher education expenses or contribute to an account to pay them. You, the account owner, name a beneficiary, which can be a child or an adult, and choose investments. Only the owner can change the beneficiary or take withdrawals, according to the MSRB.

There are two types:

The SEC explains that you can buy a savings plan directly from the state's plan or through a broker. The MSRB says plan maximums are typically between $350,000 and more than $600,000 per beneficiary.

Tax Benefits

  • Federal: contributions are not deductible, but earnings grow tax-free and withdrawals for qualified expenses are not taxed (IRS).
  • State: many states offer a deduction or credit for contributions, often only if you use your own state's plan. The SEC says just a few states let residents deduct contributions to any state's plan.
  • State definitions differ: the MSRB and Chase both note that some states do not follow the federal definition of qualified expenses, especially for K-12 costs. Check your state's rules before withdrawing for K-12.

What a 529 Plan Can Pay For

ApprenticeshipsFees, books, supplies and equipment for certain apprenticeship programs

The K-12 category now goes beyond tuition. The IRS lists curriculum and instructional materials, tutoring outside the home, standardized test, AP and admissions exam fees, dual enrollment fees, and educational therapies for students with disabilities. According to my529 and Chase, these K-12 items and the credential programs, such as those recognized under the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act or professional licenses, became qualified as of July 5, 2025, after the July 4, 2025 law known as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. The higher $20,000 K-12 limit began January 1, 2026.

Non-Qualified Withdrawals

If you withdraw money for something other than a qualified expense, the earnings portion is subject to federal income tax plus an additional 10% federal tax. Your contributions come out tax- and penalty-free because you made them with after-tax money. The MSRB says the 10% additional tax does not apply in some cases, such as when the beneficiary receives a scholarship, attends a U.S. military academy, dies or becomes disabled, although income tax on earnings still applies.

If the Money Is Not Needed

  • Change the beneficiary to an eligible family member of the current beneficiary, such as a sibling, parent, spouse, child or first cousin (MSRB).
  • Roll over to a Roth IRA for the beneficiary. The IRS says the 529 account must have been open at least 15 years, the transfer must go trustee-to-trustee, it counts toward the Roth IRA annual contribution limit, the lifetime limit is $35,000, and contributions made in the last five years (plus their earnings) cannot be rolled over. See 2026 contribution limits for the IRA limit.
  • Roll over to an ABLE account for the same beneficiary, an option the 2025 law made permanent (my529).

529 Plans and Financial Aid

The MSRB says a parent-owned 529 savings account counts as a parental asset on the FAFSA, and at most 5.64% of parental assets count toward the family's expected contribution. Distributions from grandparent-owned 529 plans are no longer counted as student income. The MSRB also notes that 529 savings may affect scholarships.

Contributions and Gift Tax

There is no IRS annual contribution limit for 529 plans; the IRS says contributions cannot exceed what is needed for the beneficiary's qualified expenses, and each plan sets its own maximum. Contributions count as gifts. For 2026, the annual gift tax exclusion is $19,000 per recipient (IRS). Chase notes you can elect to spread a larger gift over five years, putting up to $95,000 per beneficiary ($190,000 for a married couple) into a 529 at once in 2026.

Fees to Check

Savings plans can charge enrollment, annual maintenance and asset management fees, plus the expenses of the underlying funds. The SEC says plans sold through brokers may also carry sales loads and annual distribution fees of between 0.25% and 1.00%; plans you buy directly from the state avoid sales loads. The MSRB says you can change your existing investment options twice per calendar year. Before you open an account, compare your home state's plan, because of possible state tax benefits, with a lower-cost plan from another state. Frequently Asked Questions

What is a 529 plan in simple terms?

A 529 plan is a state-sponsored account for education savings. Earnings grow tax-free, and withdrawals are not taxed when they pay for qualified education expenses.

How much can you use from a 529 plan for K-12 in 2026?

Up to $20,000 per year per beneficiary for qualified K-12 expenses, up from $10,000 before 2026. Some states do not treat K-12 expenses as qualified for state tax purposes.

What happens if you use 529 money for something else?

The earnings portion of a non-qualified withdrawal is subject to federal income tax plus an additional 10% federal tax. Your contributions come back tax-free.

Can you roll a 529 plan into a Roth IRA?

Yes, up to a $35,000 lifetime limit for the beneficiary's Roth IRA, if the 529 account has been open at least 15 years. Rollovers count toward the annual Roth IRA contribution limit and must be direct transfers.

Is there a limit on how much you can put in a 529 plan?

There is no IRS annual contribution limit, but each plan sets a maximum per beneficiary, typically between $350,000 and more than $600,000 according to the MSRB. Contributions also count as gifts, and the 2026 annual gift tax exclusion is $19,000 per recipient.
