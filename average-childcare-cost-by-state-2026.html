Average Childcare Cost by State (2026)

📅 Updated August 2026 ⏱ 8 min read ✍️ By Mike Van Kempen 📊 Source: Childcare cost surveys

Infant daycare now averages about $1,230 a month — roughly $14,760 a year — making childcare one of the largest line items in any young family's budget. But what you actually pay swings enormously by state: from about $650/month in Mississippi to over $2,000/month in Washington D.C. 📌 Key Insight

The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services defines "affordable" childcare as no more than 7% of household income. In reality, infant care eats up 15–25% of a typical family's income in most states — often making it the single biggest monthly expense, ahead of rent or a mortgage. That gap is why so many families weigh whether a second income even covers the cost of care.

How to Lower Your Childcare Costs

  • Use a Dependent Care FSA — pay up to $5,000 of childcare with pre-tax dollars if your employer offers one.
  • Claim the Child and Dependent Care Tax Credit on your federal return.
  • Consider a nanny-share or home-based daycare to cut 20–50% versus a center or solo nanny.
  • Ask about discounts — many providers offer sibling, full-time, or military rates.
  • Check state subsidies and pre-K programs — eligibility and free pre-K vary widely by state.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the average cost of childcare in 2026? ▼

Infant daycare averages about $1,230/month nationally (~$14,760/year). Center-based care is ~$13,935/year; home-based is cheaper at ~$11,992. Costs drop as kids get older.

Which state is most and least expensive? ▼

Washington D.C. is highest (~$24,243/year, ~$2,020/month), then Massachusetts (~$20,913) and California. Mississippi is lowest (~$650/month), with Arkansas and Alabama also low.

How much of my income will childcare take? ▼

Typically 15–25% of household income for infant care — far above the 7% federal affordability benchmark. For many families it's the single biggest monthly expense.

Is home daycare cheaper than a center? ▼

Yes — home-based care runs 20–30% less (~$11,992 vs ~$13,935/year for infants). A full-time nanny is the priciest option ($2,500–$3,500/month plus payroll taxes); a nanny-share splits that.

How can I lower my childcare costs? ▼

Use a Dependent Care FSA (up to $5,000 pre-tax), claim the Child and Dependent Care Tax Credit, consider a nanny-share or home daycare, ask about sibling/full-time discounts, and check state subsidies and free pre-K.
