What Is a Brokerage Account? How It Works, Types and Costs

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 7 min read

General information, not investment advice. Based on SEC investor guidance at Investor.gov, FINRA and SIPC.

A brokerage account is an investment account at a registered brokerage firm that lets you buy and sell investments such as stocks, bonds, mutual funds and exchange-traded funds (ETFs). You choose between a cash account, where you pay in full for what you buy, and a margin account, where the firm can lend you money using your investments as collateral. Below: how each works, what it costs, how your account is protected and how to open or move one.

Key Takeaways

  • A brokerage account is an investment account at a registered brokerage firm that lets you buy and sell stocks, bonds, mutual funds and ETFs, according to the SEC's Investor.gov.
  • In a cash account you must pay the full amount for securities you buy; in a margin account the firm lends you money and uses your securities as collateral.
  • Under Federal Reserve Regulation T you may borrow up to 50% of the purchase price of margin securities, and FINRA requires at least 25% equity to be maintained, though many firms require 30% to 40%.
  • SIPC protects up to $500,000 per customer at a failed member brokerage firm, including a $250,000 limit for cash; it does not protect against investments losing value.
  • Account transfers between firms run through the ACATS system and generally take about three to four business days, according to FINRA.

How a Brokerage Account Works

You open the account with a brokerage firm, move money in from your bank, and then place orders to buy or sell investments. The firm executes the trade and holds the securities in your account.

Unlike a bank deposit, money you invest can lose value. A brokerage account is a container: your results depend on what you buy inside it. Common holdings include individual stocks, bonds, index funds and other mutual funds and ETFs.

A standard brokerage account is a taxable account. That means you generally report dividends, interest and any gains you realize when you sell. Retirement accounts such as IRAs, often held at the same brokerage firms, follow separate tax rules; see Roth IRA vs. traditional IRA.

Cash Account vs. Margin Account

Investor.gov describes two general types of brokerage accounts. FINRA notes that a margin account is the only type in which investors can sell short (bet on a price falling by selling borrowed shares).

FeatureCash accountMargin account
How you payThe full purchase price, with your own moneyPart with your money, part borrowed from the firm
BorrowingNot allowedUp to 50% of the purchase price of margin securities (Regulation T)
InterestNoneYou pay interest on the loan
Short sellingNot allowedAllowed
Most you can loseWhat you investedMore than you invested

Margin Rules and Risks

The SEC's investor bulletin on margin accounts lists the key rules. Before you can trade on margin, FINRA requires you to deposit at least $2,000 or 100% of the purchase price of the margin securities, whichever is less, and your firm may require more. After you buy, FINRA's maintenance requirement says your equity must stay at or above 25% of the market value of the margin securities. The SEC says many firms set it higher, typically 30% to 40%.

If your equity drops below that level, the firm can issue a margin call, asking you to deposit cash or securities right away. The SEC warns that the firm can also sell securities in your account to cover the shortfall without telling you first, and that you can lose more money than you invested.

Check your application: the SEC notes that some account applications make margin the default account type. If you do not want to borrow, choose a cash account when you sign up.

How to Open a Brokerage Account

FINRA says opening a brokerage account does not take long but needs a few more steps than opening a bank account. Expect to provide:

  • Your Social Security number or other tax identification number
  • Your age, employment status and tax status
  • Your financial situation, other investments and needs
  • Your investment experience and objectives

You then pick the account type (cash or margin), link a bank account and fund it. When you buy in a cash account, FINRA says settlement, when the trade becomes final, generally happens one day after you place the order. What a Brokerage Account Costs

FINRA says brokerage fees vary by firm. The main costs to check are:

  • Commissions: a charge per trade, where the firm applies one.
  • Account fees: standardized fees charged monthly or yearly at some firms.
  • Fund expenses: the expense ratio charged by each mutual fund or ETF you own, separate from the broker's fees. See ETF vs. mutual fund.
  • Margin interest: charged only if you borrow in a margin account.
  • Transfer fees: Investor.gov advises checking what you will pay before you move an account.

Ask for the firm's fee schedule before you open the account and read it again if you plan to use margin or move the account later.

Is Your Money Protected? SIPC Explained

If your brokerage firm is a member of the Securities Investor Protection Corporation (SIPC) and it fails financially, SIPC protects customers' cash and securities up to $500,000, which includes a $250,000 limit for cash.

SIPC protectsSIPC does not protect
Stocks, bonds and other securities held at a failed member firmA drop in the value of your investments
Cash held at the firm, up to $250,000 within the $500,000 limitLosses from bad investment advice or unsuitable recommendations
Money market mutual funds (treated as securities)Commodity futures, foreign exchange trades and unregistered investment contracts, including unregistered digital asset investment contracts

SIPC itself stresses that its protection is not the same as FDIC insurance on bank deposits. It restores missing assets when a firm fails; it does not cover market losses.

Moving a Brokerage Account to Another Firm

To switch firms, you usually fill out a transfer form with the new firm, which sends it to your old firm through the Automated Customer Account Transfer Service (ACATS), run by the National Securities Clearing Corporation. FINRA says validation and delivery generally take about three to four business days, and IRA or custodial account transfers may take longer. Investor.gov notes that if the old firm does not act on the request or a problem is not fixed within two business days, the request is purged and the new firm has to start over.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a brokerage account in simple terms?

A brokerage account is an investment account at a registered brokerage firm that you use to buy and sell investments such as stocks, bonds, mutual funds and ETFs.

What is the difference between a cash account and a margin account?

In a cash account you pay the full price of what you buy with your own money. In a margin account the firm can lend you part of the price, using your securities as collateral, and you pay interest on the loan.

Is money in a brokerage account insured?

Not like a bank deposit. If a SIPC-member brokerage firm fails, SIPC protects up to $500,000 per customer, including up to $250,000 in cash. SIPC does not cover losses when your investments fall in value.

Can you lose more than you invest in a brokerage account?

In a cash account, the most you can lose is what you invested. In a margin account you can lose more than you invested, because you owe the loan and interest even if your investments fall.

How long does it take to transfer a brokerage account?

FINRA says transfers through ACATS generally take about three to four business days. IRA and custodial account transfers may take longer.
