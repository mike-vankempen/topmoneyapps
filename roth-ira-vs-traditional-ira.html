Roth IRA vs. Traditional IRA: Which Is Better for You?

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 8 min read

General information, not tax or investment advice. Confirm details with the IRS and your plan or account provider.

The difference between a Roth IRA and a traditional IRA is when you pay income tax. With a traditional IRA, contributions may be tax-deductible now and withdrawals are taxed later; with a Roth IRA, you contribute after-tax money and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. Both share one 2026 limit of $7,500 ($8,600 if you are 50 or older). The better choice usually depends on whether you expect your tax rate to be higher now or in retirement.

Key Takeaways

  • Traditional IRA contributions may be deductible and withdrawals are taxed; Roth IRA contributions are not deductible and qualified distributions are tax-free.
  • For 2026 you can contribute up to $7,500 in total across traditional and Roth IRAs, or $8,600 if you are 50 or older (IRS).
  • Roth IRA eligibility phases out at modified AGI of $153,000 to $168,000 for single filers and $242,000 to $252,000 for married couples filing jointly in 2026.
  • Traditional IRAs require minimum distributions starting at age 73; Roth IRAs have no required minimum distributions while the owner is alive.
  • Schwab notes a Roth may be worth considering if your tax bracket could be higher in retirement, and a traditional IRA may appeal if you expect a lower bracket.

How the Tax Difference Works

A traditional IRA gives you a possible tax break today. If you qualify for the deduction, the money you put in lowers your taxable income for the year. You pay income tax later, when you take the money out in retirement.

A Roth IRA flips that. You contribute money you have already paid tax on, so there is no deduction now. In exchange, qualified distributions, including the investment growth, are not taxed. The IRS describes qualified distributions as those made after age 59½ and after the 5-year period, among other conditions.

Here is a simple hypothetical. Say you have $5,000 of pre-tax earnings, your tax rate is 22% both now and in retirement, and your investments double before you withdraw:

StepTraditional IRARoth IRA
Tax paid today$0 (deducted)$1,100
Amount invested$5,000$3,900
After the investment doubles$10,000$7,800
Tax at withdrawal (22%)$2,200$0
You keep$7,800$7,800

When the tax rate is the same at both ends, the result is the same. What tips the decision is a change in your rate. If your rate in retirement is higher than today, the Roth comes out ahead; if it is lower, the traditional IRA does. Schwab puts it this way: a Roth IRA may be worth considering if you think your tax bracket could be higher in retirement, while a traditional IRA may be more appealing if you expect a lower bracket, because it may give you a deduction now. 2026 Roth IRA Income Limits

You can contribute the full amount to a Roth IRA only if your modified AGI is below the bottom of the phase-out range. Inside the range, your allowed contribution shrinks. Above it, you cannot contribute directly.

Filing status2026 phase-out range
Single or head of household$153,000 – $168,000
Married filing jointly$242,000 – $252,000
Married filing separately$0 – $10,000

2026 Traditional IRA Deduction Limits

Anyone with taxable compensation can contribute to a traditional IRA. Whether you can deduct the contribution depends on your income only if you or your spouse is covered by a retirement plan at work. If neither of you has a workplace plan, the deduction is not limited by these ranges.

Situation2026 deduction phase-out (MAGI)

Married filing separately, covered$0 – $10,000

If your income is above these ranges, you can still make a nondeductible traditional IRA contribution. You would simply get no deduction for it.

Withdrawal Rules and the 5-Year Rule

  • Traditional IRA: withdrawals are taxed as income. Taxable distributions before age 59½ may also face a 10% additional tax unless an exception applies.
  • Roth IRA contributions: you can withdraw the money you put in at any time, tax- and penalty-free, according to Fidelity and Schwab.
  • Roth IRA earnings: to take earnings out tax-free, you generally need to be 59½ or older and meet the 5-year rule. Fidelity explains that the 5-year clock starts on January 1 of the tax year of your first Roth contribution.
  • Exceptions: Schwab lists penalty exceptions that include a qualified first-time home purchase (up to a $10,000 lifetime limit), disability, and distributions to your beneficiary after your death.

Watch the ordering: Fidelity notes that Roth withdrawals come out in a set order: regular contributions first, then converted and rollover amounts, and earnings last. That order is why many people can pull contributions out without tax.

Required Minimum Distributions

Traditional IRAs require you to start taking withdrawals, called required minimum distributions (RMDs), generally when you reach age 73. Roth IRAs do not require withdrawals while the owner is alive, although beneficiaries who inherit a Roth IRA are subject to RMD rules. If you want to leave money untouched for as long as possible, that is a point in the Roth's favor. Our 2026 RMD guide covers the details.

You do not have to pick only one. The $7,500 limit applies to the combined amount you put into traditional and Roth IRAs, so you can split it, for example $3,750 in each. Your contributions also cannot exceed your taxable compensation for the year.

Converting a Traditional IRA to a Roth

The IRS allows you to convert traditional IRA money to a Roth IRA regardless of your income. The converted amount is generally taxable in the year of the conversion, so a large conversion can push you into a higher bracket. Each conversion also has its own 5-year clock for penalty-free withdrawal of the converted amount if you are under 59½, according to Fidelity. Conversions made after 2017 cannot be undone (recharacterized).

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the main difference between a Roth IRA and a traditional IRA?

The main difference is when you pay income tax. Traditional IRA contributions may be tax-deductible now, and withdrawals are taxed in retirement. Roth IRA contributions are made with after-tax money, and qualified distributions, including earnings, are tax-free.

What is the IRA contribution limit for 2026?

For 2026 you can contribute up to $7,500 in total to traditional and Roth IRAs combined. If you are 50 or older, you can add $1,100, for a total of $8,600. Your contributions cannot exceed your taxable compensation for the year.

What are the 2026 Roth IRA income limits?

The ability to contribute to a Roth IRA phases out at modified AGI of $153,000 to $168,000 for single and head-of-household filers and $242,000 to $252,000 for married couples filing jointly. For married filing separately the range is $0 to $10,000.

Can I contribute to both a Roth IRA and a traditional IRA in the same year?

Yes. You can split your contributions between the two, but the $7,500 limit ($8,600 at age 50 or older) applies to the combined total, not to each account.

Do Roth IRAs have required minimum distributions?

No. Roth IRAs have no required minimum distributions while the owner is alive. Traditional IRAs generally require withdrawals starting at age 73. Beneficiaries who inherit a Roth IRA are subject to RMD rules.
