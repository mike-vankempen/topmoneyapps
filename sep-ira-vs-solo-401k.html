SEP IRA vs. Solo 401(k): Limits, Rules and Trade-Offs

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 8 min read

General information, not tax or investment advice. Rules from IRS Publication 560 and the IRS retirement plan pages; a tax professional can run your exact numbers.

Both plans share the same $72,000 cap for 2026, but a solo 401(k) usually lets you reach it on less income because it adds employee deferrals of up to $24,500 (plus catch-ups) on top of the employer contribution. A SEP IRA allows only the employer contribution, about 20% of net self-employment earnings for a sole proprietor, but it is simpler to run and can be opened as late as your extended tax deadline.

Key Takeaways

  • For 2026, total contributions to a SEP IRA or a solo 401(k) are capped at $72,000, and only the first $360,000 of compensation counts (IRS COLA table).
  • A SEP IRA takes employer contributions only, up to 25% of each employee's pay; for a self-employed owner that works out to 20% of net earnings, figured after the deductible half of self-employment tax (IRS Publication 560).
  • A solo 401(k) adds employee elective deferrals of up to $24,500 in 2026 ($25,500 in 2027), plus an $8,000 catch-up at 50 or older ($8,500 in 2027) or $11,250 at ages 60 to 63 ($11,750 in 2027).
  • SEP IRAs do not allow catch-up contributions or loans, while a solo 401(k) can offer Roth deferrals and plan loans if the plan document allows them (IRS).
  • A solo 401(k) must file Form 5500-EZ once plan assets exceed $250,000 at year-end; a SEP generally has no annual IRS filing (IRS).

How a SEP IRA Works

A Simplified Employee Pension (SEP) is the simplest retirement plan for a business. The IRS says "only the employer contributes," up to 25% of each employee's pay, into a traditional IRA set up for each person, or a Roth SEP IRA if the plan chooses to accept Roth contributions.

If you are self-employed, you are both the employer and the employee, so the math changes. The IRS SEP FAQs say to base your contribution on net profit, minus one-half of your self-employment tax, minus the SEP contribution itself. Publication 560 turns that into a rate table: when the plan rate is 25%, your own rate is 20% of net earnings from self-employment, figured after the deduction for half of self-employment tax.

Other SEP rules from the IRS:

  • No catch-ups. The SEP FAQs say catch-up contributions apply only to employee elective deferrals, which a SEP does not have.
  • No loans. The IRS lists participant loans as "not permitted."
  • Light paperwork. You can adopt the IRS model Form 5305-SEP, which is not filed with the IRS, and the employer generally has no annual filing, including Form 5500.
  • Late set-up. You can set up a SEP for a year as late as the due date of your business's return, including extensions, and deposit contributions by that same date.

How a Solo 401(k) Works

The IRS calls it a one-participant 401(k): "a traditional 401(k) plan covering a business owner with no employees, or that person and his or her spouse." You contribute in two roles:

  • As the employee: elective deferrals up to 100% of compensation, capped at $24,500 for 2026 and $25,500 for 2027, plus catch-ups if you are 50 or older.
  • As the employer: a nonelective contribution of up to 25% of compensation as defined by the plan; for a self-employed owner, Publication 560 uses the same 20%-of-net-earnings calculation as a SEP.

The deferral limit is per person, not per plan. The IRS says elective deferral limits apply across all plans you take part in during the year, so if you also defer $20,000 into a 401(k) at a day job, you have only $4,500 of 2026 deferral room left for your solo 401(k).

The IRS also notes that deferrals can be made pre-tax or as designated Roth contributions, and plans can allow loans and hardship distributions. Owners must elect deferrals by the end of the tax year, and the money can then be deposited by the tax filing deadline, including extensions, according to Publication 560. For a new plan, Publication 560 says a sole proprietor with no employees can adopt a 401(k) after the end of the tax year, as long as it is adopted by the filing deadline (without extensions).

The extra work: once plan assets total more than $250,000 at the end of the plan year, you generally must file Form 5500-EZ, due by the last day of the seventh month after the plan year ends.

Your actual limit depends on your net profit, self-employment tax and any deferrals at other jobs; Publication 560 has the worksheet. At high incomes the gap narrows, because 20% of net earnings can reach the $72,000 cap on its own in either plan.

If You Have (or Hire) Employees

This is often the deciding factor. A solo 401(k) is only for an owner with no employees other than a spouse; the IRS notes that hiring employees changes the plan's testing and coverage rules. A SEP can cover employees, but the IRS requires the same contribution percentage for every eligible employee. For 2026, an employee is eligible if they are at least 21, worked for you in at least 3 of the last 5 years and earned at least $800. So a 20% contribution for yourself can mean 25% of pay for each eligible employee.

Which One Fits

  • Solo 401(k) usually allows more at low and middle incomes, adds catch-ups and Roth deferrals, and can allow loans, at the cost of more paperwork and a 5500-EZ once assets pass $250,000.
  • SEP IRA is simpler, has no annual filing, can be set up as late as your extended deadline and can cover employees, but it has no deferrals or catch-ups.
  • Day job too? Deferral room is shared with any other 401(k) you have, which can shrink the solo 401(k)'s advantage.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which allows bigger contributions, a SEP IRA or a solo 401(k)?

Both are capped at $72,000 for 2026, but a solo 401(k) usually lets you contribute more at the same income because you can add employee deferrals of up to $24,500 on top of the employer contribution. A SEP allows only the employer contribution, about 20% of net self-employment earnings for a sole proprietor.

Can I have both a SEP IRA and a solo 401(k)?

The IRS says its model SEP form, Form 5305-SEP, cannot be used if you maintain another qualified plan, such as a 401(k). Combining plans raises extra limit and paperwork questions, so check with a tax professional first.

Does a SEP IRA allow catch-up contributions?

No. The IRS says SEPs are funded by employer contributions only, and catch-up contributions apply only to employee elective deferrals. A solo 401(k) allows an $8,000 catch-up at 50 or older in 2026, or $11,250 at ages 60 to 63.

When is the deadline to open a SEP IRA or solo 401(k)?

A SEP can be set up and funded as late as your tax return due date, including extensions. A sole proprietor with no employees can adopt a solo 401(k) after year-end as long as it is adopted by the tax filing deadline, without extensions.

Do I have to file anything with the IRS for a solo 401(k)?

Yes, once plan assets are more than $250,000 at the end of the plan year, you generally must file Form 5500-EZ, due by the last day of the seventh month after the plan year ends. A SEP generally has no annual filing.

Can I use a solo 401(k) if I have employees?

Not generally. The IRS describes a one-participant 401(k) as covering a business owner with no employees, or the owner and spouse. A SEP can cover employees but must give each eligible employee the same percentage of pay.
