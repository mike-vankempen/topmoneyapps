📌 Key Insight

Housing drives almost the entire gap. Groceries, utilities, and gas vary only modestly between states, but the housing index runs from the high 60s in the cheapest states to over 300 in Hawaii. That means the same $70,000 salary can feel comfortable in Mississippi and stretched thin in Honolulu or the Bay Area — the difference is almost entirely what you pay for a roof over your head.

Does a Higher Salary Make Up for It?

High-cost states usually pay more — but not always enough to close the gap. When you adjust pay for local costs, some lower-cost states actually offer stronger real purchasing power than famous high-wage states. Frequently Asked Questions

Which state has the highest cost of living in 2026? ▼

Hawaii, at roughly 85% above the U.S. average (index ~185). Massachusetts, California, New York, and Alaska follow. Housing is the main driver.

Which state has the lowest cost of living? ▼

Mississippi and Oklahoma, around 14–15% below the national average (index ~85–86). Arkansas, Alabama, Kansas, and Missouri are also among the cheapest.

How does the cost of living index work? ▼

The U.S. average is set to 100. A state at 120 is 20% pricier than average; 85 is 15% cheaper. It blends housing, groceries, utilities, transportation, and healthcare, with housing weighted most heavily.

Why is housing the biggest factor? ▼

Housing is the largest share of most budgets and the most variable — the housing index spans from the high 60s to over 300. That's why it swings the overall index more than any other category.

Does a higher salary offset a higher cost of living? ▼

Not always. A $70,000 salary stretches much further in Mississippi than in Hawaii or California. Compare pay to local costs — our salary-by-state guide ranks states after adjusting for cost of living.
