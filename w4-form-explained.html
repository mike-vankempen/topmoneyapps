How to Fill Out a W-4 in 2026: A Step-by-Step Guide

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 8 min read

General information, not tax advice. Check your entries with the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator or a tax professional.

To fill out a W-4, complete Step 1 (name, Social Security number and filing status) and sign Step 5; fill in Steps 2 through 4 only if you have more than one job in your household, dependents, other income or deductions. The 2026 form uses $2,200 per qualifying child in Step 3, and its deductions worksheet now covers the new tips, overtime, car loan interest and senior deductions.

Key Takeaways

  • Only Step 1 (personal information) and Step 5 (signature) of Form W-4 are required; Steps 2 to 4 apply only if you have multiple jobs, dependents, other income or deductions.
  • On the 2026 Form W-4, Step 3 uses $2,200 for each qualifying child under 17 and $500 for each other dependent.
  • The 2026 W-4 deductions worksheet includes lines for qualified tips, qualified overtime, passenger vehicle loan interest and the deduction for people 65 and older (IRS Publication 505).
  • If you do not submit a W-4, your employer withholds as if you are single or married filing separately with no adjustments, according to the IRS.
  • An employer must start using a new W-4 no later than the first payroll period ending on or after the 30th day after receiving it (IRS Topic 753).

What a W-4 Does

Form W-4, the Employee's Withholding Certificate, tells your employer how much federal income tax to take out of each paycheck. The IRS says employers must have every new hire complete one. It is not sent to the IRS and it does not change how much tax you owe for the year. It only changes how much you prepay through withholding.

Get it wrong in one direction and you give the government an interest-free loan until you file. Through the week ending April 10, 2026, the IRS reported an average refund of $3,397 on 78.05 million refunds, up from $3,055 a year earlier. Get it wrong in the other direction and you can owe a lump sum, and possibly a penalty, in April. Our average tax refund by state page shows how refunds compare across the country.

If you never turn in a W-4, the IRS says your employer withholds as if you are single or married filing separately, with no other adjustments. For many married people and parents, that means more tax withheld than needed.

The 5 Steps on the 2026 Form W-4

The IRS's current form is Form W-4 (2026), and the IRS draft for 2027 keeps the same five steps. Only Step 1 and Step 5 are required. Fill in Steps 2 through 4 only if they apply to you.

Step-by-Step: How to Fill It Out

Step 1: Personal information and filing status

Enter your name, address and Social Security number, then check one filing status: single or married filing separately; married filing jointly (or qualifying surviving spouse); or head of household. Your filing status sets the standard deduction your withholding assumes. For 2026 the IRS set the standard deduction at $16,100 for single filers and married people filing separately, $32,200 for married couples filing jointly and $24,150 for heads of household. See our 2026 standard deduction guide for details.

Step 2: Multiple jobs or a working spouse

This step matters because each employer withholds as if its paycheck were your only income. Without it, two incomes can each get a full standard deduction and the lowest tax brackets, so too little is withheld. The form gives three options. Use only one:

  • (a) The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator at irs.gov/W4App. The form calls this the most accurate option, and recommends it if you have self-employment income.
  • (b) The Multiple Jobs Worksheet on page 3 of the form. You enter the result in Step 4(c) as extra withholding.
  • (c) The checkbox, for households with exactly two jobs in total. Check it on both W-4s. The IRS explains that the standard deduction and tax brackets are then cut in half for each job. It works well when the two jobs pay about the same and over-withholds when the pay is very different.

Fill out Steps 3 through 4(b) on only one W-4. The form says withholding is most accurate if you do this on the W-4 for the highest-paying job.

Step 3: Dependents and other credits

On the 2026 form, multiply the number of qualifying children under age 17 by $2,200 and the number of other dependents by $500, then add any other credits you expect, such as education credits. The $2,200 figure reflects the larger Child Tax Credit under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. The form says to complete this step if your income will be $200,000 or less ($400,000 or less if married filing jointly). The 2027 draft still shows a placeholder for the per-child amount, so check the final 2027 form when it is released.

Step 4: Other adjustments

  • 4(a) Other income: income that has no withholding, such as interest, dividends or retirement income. Do not include income from other jobs here.
  • 4(b) Deductions: use the Deductions Worksheet if you expect to itemize or claim other deductions. Under IRS Publication 505, this worksheet now covers the new deductions for qualified tips, qualified overtime, passenger vehicle loan interest and the enhanced deduction for people 65 and older. Each has its own limits and income phase-outs listed on the worksheet.
  • 4(c) Extra withholding: any additional flat amount you want withheld each pay period. This is the simplest fix if you owed money last year.

Step 5: Sign and date

Sign and date the form and give it to your employer, not the IRS. Your employer must start using it no later than the start of the first payroll period ending on or after the 30th day after it receives the form.

Shortcut: the IRS updated its Tax Withholding Estimator in March 2026 to include no tax on tips, no tax on overtime, no tax on car loan interest and the deduction for seniors. If the estimator recommends a change, it helps you fill out a new W-4. Have a recent pay stub and last year's return handy.

When to Submit a New W-4

You can change your W-4 at any time. Common reasons to review it:

  • You start a new job (required).
  • You marry, divorce, or have or adopt a child.
  • You or your spouse take a second job or stop working.
  • You start earning tips, overtime or side income, or your deductions change.
  • You got a large refund or owed a large balance last year.

Some changes are not optional. Publication 505 says that if a change reduces the withholding you are entitled to claim, you must give your employer a new W-4 within 10 days. When the IRS redesigned the form in 2020, it said existing employees did not have to submit a new form just because of the redesign.

Many states use their own withholding form, so your employer may also ask for a state version. Payroll provider Patriot Software lists, for example, California's DE 4 and New York's IT-2104, while New Mexico, North Dakota and Utah use the federal W-4.

Claiming Exempt From Withholding

You can write "Exempt" on the form only if you had no federal income tax liability last year and expect none this year. For the 2026 form, that means no liability in 2025 and none expected in 2026. An exemption lasts one year: the 2026 form says you must submit a new W-4 by February 16, 2027 to keep it, and the IRS's draft 2027 form sets a February 15, 2028 deadline for the next year. Claiming exempt when you will owe tax can leave you with a large bill.

How to Avoid an Underpayment Penalty

Withholding counts toward the IRS's "pay as you go" rules. According to the IRS, you generally avoid the underpayment penalty if any of these is true:

  • You owe less than $1,000 after subtracting withholding and refundable credits.
  • You paid at least 90% of the tax for the current year.
  • You paid at least 100% of the tax shown on last year's return, or 110% if last year's adjusted gross income was more than $150,000 ($75,000 if married filing separately).

If you expect to come up short, you can raise the extra withholding in Step 4(c). If you have income with no withholding, such as self-employment income, IRS Publication 505 explains how to make estimated tax payments instead; see the dates on our 2027 tax deadlines page.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to fill out every step of the W-4?

No. Only Step 1 (personal information and filing status) and Step 5 (signature) are required. Complete Steps 2, 3 and 4 only if they apply: multiple jobs or a working spouse, dependents, or other income, deductions or extra withholding.

How do I fill out a W-4 if my spouse and I both work?

Use one Step 2 option: the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator, the Multiple Jobs Worksheet, or the Step 2(c) checkbox on both W-4s if there are only two jobs in total. The checkbox is most accurate when the two jobs pay about the same. Complete Steps 3 to 4(b) on only one W-4, ideally for the higher-paying job.

How much do I put for dependents on the 2026 W-4?

On the 2026 form, multiply the number of qualifying children under 17 by $2,200 and the number of other dependents by $500, then add other credits you expect. The form says to complete Step 3 if your income will be $200,000 or less ($400,000 or less if married filing jointly).

What happens if I don't fill out a W-4?

Your employer withholds federal income tax as if you are single or married filing separately with no other adjustments, according to the IRS. That can mean more tax withheld than you need, especially if you are married or have children.

Can I claim exempt on my W-4?

Only if you had no federal income tax liability last year and expect none this year. The exemption lasts one year. The 2026 form says you must submit a new W-4 by February 16, 2027 to keep claiming it.

How often can I change my W-4?

You can submit a new W-4 at any time. Your employer must put it into effect no later than the first payroll period ending on or after the 30th day after receiving it. If a change reduces the withholding you can claim, you must submit a new form within 10 days.
