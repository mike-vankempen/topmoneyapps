2026 Tax Brackets and Federal Income Tax Rates

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 7 min read

General information, not tax advice. Figures are from IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32; confirm your own situation with the IRS or a tax professional.

For tax year 2026, the federal income tax has seven rates: 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35% and 37%. A single filer pays 10% on the first $12,400 of taxable income and 37% only on income over $640,600 ($768,700 for married couples filing jointly). These brackets cover income you earn in 2026 and generally apply to the return you file in 2027. Below are the full tables for every filing status and an example of how the math works.

Key Takeaways

  • The 2026 federal income tax rates are 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35% and 37%, according to IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32.
  • For 2026, the 10% bracket covers taxable income up to $12,400 for single filers and $24,800 for married couples filing jointly.
  • The 37% top rate applies to 2026 taxable income over $640,600 for single filers and heads of household, and over $768,700 for married couples filing jointly.
  • The IRS says the tax year 2026 adjustments generally apply to tax returns filed in 2027.
  • Brackets apply to taxable income, which is income after deductions such as the 2026 standard deduction of $16,100 for single filers.

How Tax Brackets Work: A 2026 Example

The U.S. uses marginal rates. Each rate applies only to the slice of income inside its bracket, not to all of your income. Moving into a higher bracket never shrinks your after-tax income; only the dollars above the threshold are taxed at the higher rate.

Example: a single filer earns $76,100 in wages in 2026 and takes the $16,100 standard deduction. Taxable income is $60,000.

  • The first $12,400 is taxed at 10%: $1,240.
  • The next $38,000 (up to $50,400) is taxed at 12%: $4,560.
  • The last $9,600 (from $50,400 to $60,000) is taxed at 22%: $2,112.

Total federal income tax: $7,912, before any credits. This filer is "in the 22% bracket," but the effective rate on taxable income is about 13.2%, and lower still on the $76,100 of wages.

Marginal vs. effective rate: your marginal rate is the rate on your next dollar of income. Your effective rate is your total tax divided by your income. The marginal rate matters for decisions like how much a 401(k) contribution saves you; the effective rate shows what you actually pay.

What Changed for 2026

The IRS announced the 2026 figures in October 2025 in news release IR-2025-103, which covers more than 60 inflation-adjusted provisions, including changes from the One, Big, Beautiful Bill (OBBB) signed in July 2025.

  • Same seven rates. The 10%–37% rate structure continues. The Tax Foundation notes that the OBBB made this bracket structure permanent and gave the 10% and 12% brackets a larger inflation adjustment than the higher brackets.
  • Higher thresholds. Every bracket threshold rose with inflation, so the same income can land in a lower slice than it did in 2025.
  • Personal exemption stays at $0. The IRS says personal exemptions remain at zero for 2026, as in 2025.
  • A cap for top earners on itemizing. The IRS says the OBBB limits the tax benefit of itemized deductions for taxpayers in the 37% bracket.

Brackets Apply to Taxable Income, Not Your Salary

Your bracket is based on taxable income: your gross income minus adjustments and either the standard deduction or your itemized deductions. For 2026, the standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers, $32,200 for married couples filing jointly and $24,150 for heads of household. See our 2026 standard deduction guide for the extra amounts for people 65 and older.

Pre-tax retirement contributions also lower taxable income. A worker in the 22% bracket who moves $1,000 into a traditional 401(k) cuts federal income tax by about $220 that year. Check the 2026 contribution limits for how much you can put in.

Long-term capital gains and qualified investment income follow a separate rate schedule of 0%, 15% and 20%. Short-term gains are taxed at the ordinary rates above. See 2026 capital gains tax rates.

These are federal brackets only. Most states add their own income tax, and a few do not tax wages at all (see states with no income tax).

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the federal tax brackets for 2026?

There are seven rates: 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35% and 37%. For single filers the brackets start at $0, $12,401, $50,401, $105,701, $201,776, $256,226 and $640,601 of taxable income. For married couples filing jointly they start at $0, $24,801, $100,801, $211,401, $403,551, $512,451 and $768,701.

When do the 2026 tax brackets apply?

They apply to income earned from January 1 to December 31, 2026. The IRS says the tax year 2026 adjustments generally apply to tax returns filed in 2027.

Does moving into a higher tax bracket lower my take-home pay?

No. Each rate applies only to the income inside that bracket. If a raise pushes you into the 22% bracket, only the dollars above the 22% threshold are taxed at 22%; everything below is taxed at the lower rates as before.

What is the top tax rate in 2026?

The top federal income tax rate for 2026 is 37%. It applies to taxable income over $640,600 for single filers and heads of household, $768,700 for married couples filing jointly and $384,350 for married couples filing separately.

Is there a personal exemption in 2026?

No. The IRS says personal exemptions remain at $0 for tax year 2026, as in 2025. The separate deduction of up to $6,000 for people 65 and older is not a personal exemption.
