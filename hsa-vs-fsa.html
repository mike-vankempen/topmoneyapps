HSA vs FSA (2026): Which Should You Choose?

📅 Updated November 2026 ⏱ 6 min read ✍️ Mike Van Kempen

📋 General information, not tax advice. Confirm specifics with the IRS and your plan documents.

It's open-enrollment season, and the HSA-vs-FSA box trips a lot of people up. The short version: if you're eligible for an HSA, it's usually the stronger account — it rolls over forever, can be invested, and you keep it for life. An FSA is simpler but use-it-or-lose-it and tied to your employer. Here's the full 2026 comparison and how to decide.

Key Takeaways

  • An HSA (Health Savings Account) is paired with a high-deductible health plan, rolls over year to year with no limit, can be invested, and is yours to keep even if you change jobs.
  • For 2026, the HSA limit is $4,400 for self-only coverage and $8,750 for family coverage, plus a $1,000 catch-up if you're 55 or older.
  • FSA funds are generally forfeited at year-end — 'use it or lose it.' Employers may offer one of two softeners: a carryover of up to $680 into the next year, or a grace period of up to 2.5 months to spend the prior year's balance.

When the HSA Wins

If you have an HSA-eligible high-deductible health plan (2026 minimum deductibles: $1,700 self-only / $3,400 family), the HSA is hard to beat. It's the only account that's pre-tax going in, tax-free growing, and tax-free coming out for medical costs. Because it rolls over and can be invested, many people treat a maxed HSA as a stealth retirement account — after age 65 you can withdraw for any reason (paying only income tax, like a traditional IRA), while medical withdrawals stay tax-free.

Power move: if you can afford to, pay current medical bills out of pocket and let the HSA stay invested for decades. Those tax-free gains compound into one of the best deals in the tax code.

When the FSA Makes Sense

If you don't have an HSA-eligible plan — most PPO and low-deductible plans don't qualify — the FSA is your pre-tax option for health costs. It shines when you have predictable expenses you'll definitely spend within the year: regular prescriptions, planned dental or vision work, or ongoing therapy. The catch is the "use it or lose it" rule, so fund it to match costs you're confident about.

Don't over-fund an FSA. Unspent money is generally forfeited. Employers may offer a carryover of up to $680 or a grace period of up to 2.5 months — one or the other, not both. Check which (if any) your plan allows before you decide your contribution.

Can You Have Both?

Generally, no — contributing to an HSA and a general-purpose health FSA at the same time isn't allowed, because the FSA counts as disqualifying coverage. The exception is a limited-purpose FSA (dental and vision only) or a post-deductible FSA, which can be paired with an HSA. If your employer offers a limited-purpose FSA, you can run both.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between an HSA and an FSA?

An HSA (with a high-deductible plan) rolls over forever, can be invested, and is yours for life. An FSA works with most plans but is use-it-or-lose-it, employer-owned, with only a small carryover.

What are the 2026 HSA and FSA limits?

HSA: $4,400 self-only / $8,750 family (+$1,000 at 55+). Health FSA: $3,400, with up to $680 carryover if your employer allows it.

Can I have both an HSA and an FSA?

Generally no — a general-purpose FSA disqualifies HSA contributions. The exception is a limited-purpose (dental/vision) or post-deductible FSA, which can pair with an HSA.

Which is better?

If you're HSA-eligible, usually the HSA — higher limits, no forfeiture, invested growth, kept for life. The FSA is best when you lack an HSA-eligible plan but have predictable yearly costs.

What happens to unspent FSA money?

Generally forfeited. Employers may allow a $680 carryover or a 2.5-month grace period (one, not both). Estimate conservatively.
