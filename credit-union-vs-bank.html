Credit Union vs. Bank: Key Differences, Rates and Insurance

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 7 min read

General information, not financial advice. Based on NCUA, MyCreditUnion.gov and FDIC data, checked November 2026.

The main difference is ownership: a credit union is a not-for-profit cooperative owned by its members, while most banks are for-profit companies owned by shareholders. In NCUA's latest rate comparison (Q4 2025), credit unions averaged lower rates on car loans and credit cards and higher CD rates than banks, but you must qualify for membership, and both are federally insured up to $250,000.

Key Takeaways

  • Credit unions are not-for-profit organizations owned and controlled by their members and run by a member-elected volunteer board (MyCreditUnion.gov).
  • Deposits at federally insured credit unions are covered up to $250,000 by the NCUA's share insurance fund, matching the FDIC's $250,000 standard limit for banks; both are backed by the full faith and credit of the United States.
  • In NCUA's Q4 2025 comparison, 60-month new car loans averaged 5.44% at credit unions vs. 7.41% at banks, and 5-year CDs paid 2.83% vs. 2.11%.
  • Federal credit unions face a loan interest rate ceiling of 18%, which the NCUA Board extended on February 6, 2026 through September 10, 2027.
  • As of June 30, 2026 there were 4,214 federally insured credit unions with 146.1 million members, and 4,238 FDIC-insured banks and savings institutions.

Ownership and Membership

MyCreditUnion.gov, the NCUA's consumer site, puts it plainly: "All credit unions are not-for-profit organizations" and "Credit unions are owned and controlled by their members." When you open an account, you become a member-owner. Surplus income goes back to members, as dividends.

The catch is eligibility. Each credit union serves a field of membership, meaning a common bond among its members. You might qualify through your employer, a family member, where you live, work, worship or go to school, or a group such as a church, school, labor union or homeowners' association. The NCUA's online mapping tool helps you find credit unions you may be able to join.

Banks generally have no membership requirement. Many credit unions use shared branching to widen access: the CO-OP network says it has over 5,600 shared branches and over 30,000 surcharge-free ATMs that members of participating credit unions can use.

Is Your Money Safe? NCUA vs. FDIC

Yes, at either one, if the institution is federally insured. The NCUA insures share accounts at federally insured credit unions up to $250,000 through the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund (NCUSIF), which is "backed by the full faith and credit of the United States." The NCUA says, "No one has lost a single penny of insured deposits at a federally insured credit union."

Banks are covered by the FDIC up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category, and the FDIC says no depositor has lost a penny of FDIC-insured funds since it was founded in 1933. Our FDIC insurance guide explains ownership categories in detail.

Check the insurer: the NCUA notes that some state-chartered credit unions use private insurance, which is not backed by the government. Neither NCUA nor FDIC insurance covers stocks, bonds, mutual funds, annuities or life insurance, even when sold at a credit union or bank.

Loan Rate Cap and Small-Dollar Loans

Federal law sets a 15% interest rate ceiling on loans by federal credit unions, but the NCUA Board can raise it temporarily. On February 6, 2026, the Board kept the ceiling at 18% and extended it through September 10, 2027. This applies to federal credit unions, not state-chartered ones.

Federal credit unions can also offer payday alternative loans (PALs) under NCUA rules:

  • PALs I: $200 to $1,000, terms of one to six months, and you must have been a member for at least one month.
  • PALs II: up to $2,000, terms of one to 12 months, with no membership waiting period.
  • Both: an application fee of no more than $20, and no more than three PALs per borrower in any rolling six months, one at a time.

Tax Status

An NCUA legal opinion states that "due to their not-for-profit, cooperative nature, FCUs are exempt from both federal and state income taxes," citing the Internal Revenue Code and the Federal Credit Union Act. The opinion covers federal credit unions; state-chartered credit unions are outside its scope.

Which Should You Choose?

A credit union may fit if you qualify for membership and you plan to borrow, for example for a car loan or a credit card, since NCUA's averages favored credit unions on loan rates. A bank may fit if you want a large branch or ATM network, a wide product lineup, or you do not qualify for a credit union. You can also use both, for example a credit union for a car loan and a bank for savings. Whichever you pick, confirm it is federally insured, read the fee schedule, and see our SoFi vs. Chime vs. Ally comparison for app-based banking options.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the main difference between a credit union and a bank?

Ownership. A credit union is a not-for-profit cooperative owned by its members and run by a volunteer board they elect. Most banks are for-profit companies owned by shareholders.

Is money safer in a credit union or a bank?

Both are equally protected if federally insured. Federally insured credit unions are covered by the NCUA up to $250,000 and banks by the FDIC up to $250,000, and both are backed by the full faith and credit of the United States.

Do credit unions have better rates than banks?

On average, often for loans. In NCUA's Q4 2025 comparison, credit unions averaged 5.44% on 60-month new car loans vs. 7.41% at banks and 12.58% on classic credit cards vs. 15.27%. Banks averaged more on regular savings, 0.32% vs. 0.19%.

Can anyone join a credit union?

Only people in its field of membership, such as those tied to an employer, a family member, a community, a school or a group. Many credit unions have broad fields of membership, and the NCUA offers a tool to find ones you may qualify for.

What is the maximum interest rate a federal credit union can charge?

18% on most loans. The NCUA Board extended this temporary ceiling on February 6, 2026 through September 10, 2027; the ceiling set in law is 15%.
