2026 Standard Deduction by Filing Status

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 7 min read

General information, not tax advice. Figures are from IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32; confirm your own situation with the IRS or a tax professional.

The 2026 standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers and married people filing separately, $32,200 for married couples filing jointly and $24,150 for heads of household. If you are 65 or older or blind, you add $1,650 per condition ($2,050 if you are unmarried). These amounts apply to tax year 2026, which you report on the return you file in 2027.

2026 Standard Deduction Amounts

The standard deduction is a flat dollar amount that reduces the income you pay tax on. The IRS adjusts it for inflation every year. Here are the 2026 amounts next to 2025 for comparison.

Filing status20252026Change
Single$15,750$16,100+$350
Married filing jointly / surviving spouse$31,500$32,200+$700
Head of household$23,625$24,150+$525
Married filing separately$15,750$16,100+$350

Example: a married couple filing jointly with $90,000 of income and no other adjustments subtracts $32,200 and pays federal income tax on $57,800. See how that taxable income is taxed in our 2026 tax brackets guide.

Extra Standard Deduction for Age 65+ or Blindness

If you are 65 or older or blind at the end of the tax year, you get an additional standard deduction on top of the basic amount. For 2026 it is $1,650 per condition for married people and $2,050 if you are unmarried and not a surviving spouse. Someone who is both 65+ and blind gets two additional amounts.

Situation (2026)BasicAdditionalTotal
Single, 65 or older$16,100$2,050$18,150
Single, 65 or older and blind$16,100$4,100$20,200
Head of household, 65 or older$24,150$2,050$26,200
Married filing jointly, one spouse 65+$32,200$1,650$33,850
Married filing jointly, both spouses 65+$32,200$3,300$35,500

The IRS treats you as 65 on the day before your 65th birthday. For the blindness amount, you must be blind on the last day of the tax year. You claim either one by checking the boxes on Form 1040 or Form 1040-SR.

The Separate $6,000 Deduction for Seniors

Starting with 2025, the One, Big, Beautiful Bill added a separate deduction for people 65 and older. The IRS describes it as follows:

  • Up to $6,000 per eligible person, or $12,000 for a married couple when both spouses qualify.
  • It is in addition to the regular additional standard deduction for seniors described above.
  • It is available whether you itemize or take the standard deduction.
  • It phases out for modified adjusted gross income over $75,000 ($150,000 for joint filers).
  • It runs from tax year 2025 through 2028, requires a valid Social Security number, and married couples must file jointly to claim it.

Example: a married couple, both 65+, with income below the phase-out could take the $32,200 standard deduction, $3,300 in additional standard deduction and up to $12,000 from the senior deduction, for up to $47,500 in total deductions for 2026.

Standard Deduction for Dependents

If someone else can claim you as a dependent, such as a teenager with a part-time job, your 2026 standard deduction is limited to the greater of $1,350 or your earned income plus $450, but never more than the regular amount for your filing status.

Example: a dependent student who earns $5,000 from a summer job gets a standard deduction of $5,450 ($5,000 + $450). A dependent with $8,000 of investment income and no earned income gets $1,350.

Standard Deduction vs. Itemizing

You take either the standard deduction or your itemized deductions, not both. Itemized deductions include mortgage interest, state and local taxes, charitable gifts and large medical expenses, reported on Schedule A. Most people should take whichever is larger.

A few people cannot use the standard deduction at all, according to IRS Topic 551:

  • A married person filing separately whose spouse itemizes.
  • Most nonresident aliens and dual-status aliens (with some exceptions).
  • Someone filing a return for less than 12 months because of a change in accounting period.
  • Estates, trusts, common trust funds and partnerships.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the standard deduction for 2026?

For tax year 2026, the standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers and married people filing separately, $32,200 for married couples filing jointly and surviving spouses, and $24,150 for heads of household.

How much is the extra standard deduction for seniors in 2026?

If you are 65 or older or blind, you add $1,650 per condition if married, or $2,050 if unmarried and not a surviving spouse. A single filer who is 65 or older has a total standard deduction of $18,150 for 2026.

Can I take the $6,000 senior deduction if I don't itemize?

Yes. The IRS says the deduction of up to $6,000 for people 65 and older is available to both itemizers and non-itemizers. It phases out for modified adjusted gross income over $75,000 ($150,000 for joint filers) and runs from 2025 through 2028.

What is the standard deduction for a dependent in 2026?

If someone can claim you as a dependent, your 2026 standard deduction is the greater of $1,350 or your earned income plus $450, but no more than the regular standard deduction for your filing status.

Should I take the standard deduction or itemize?

Take whichever is larger. If your mortgage interest, state and local taxes, charitable gifts and other itemized deductions add up to more than your standard deduction, itemizing lowers your tax more. You cannot claim both.
