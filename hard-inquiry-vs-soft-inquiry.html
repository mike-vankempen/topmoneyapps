Hard vs. Soft Credit Inquiries: What's the Difference?

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 7 min read

Based on CFPB, myFICO, VantageScore and Experian, checked November 2026.

A hard inquiry happens when a lender checks your credit because you applied for credit, such as a card, auto loan or mortgage, and it can lower your score. A soft inquiry happens when you check your own credit, get a prescreened offer, or an existing lender reviews your account, and the CFPB says soft inquiries do not affect your scores. For most people, one extra hard inquiry costs less than five FICO points, and FICO counts it for only 12 months.

Key Takeaways

  • Hard inquiries come from applications for credit, such as a credit card, a credit line increase, an auto loan or a mortgage, and can lower your credit score.
  • Soft inquiries, including checking your own credit, prescreened offers, employment screening and reviews by your existing lenders, do not affect your credit scores, according to the CFPB.
  • For most people, one additional hard inquiry lowers a FICO Score by less than five points, according to myFICO.
  • Hard inquiries can stay on your credit report for up to two years, but FICO Scores only consider inquiries from the last 12 months.
  • Newer FICO Scores count multiple mortgage, auto or student loan inquiries within 45 days as one; VantageScore uses a 14-day window, and credit card applications are not grouped.

What Is a Hard Inquiry?

Experian describes a hard inquiry as "a record that your credit report was accessed in response to an application you submitted." The CFPB says lenders make hard inquiries after you apply for credit, and that these inquiries will affect your credit score. myFICO lists applying for a credit card, a credit card line increase, an auto loan or a mortgage as common triggers.

A lender needs a legal reason to pull your report. Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA), a report can be used only for a "permissible purpose," such as a credit transaction involving you or your written instructions.

What Is a Soft Inquiry?

The CFPB lists these as soft inquiries: reviews of existing accounts by lenders or insurance companies, prescreening by prospective lenders, employment screening and your own requests for your credit reports. It says these will not affect your credit scores. myFICO adds that checking your report through AnnualCreditReport.com, or with a tool like myFICO, is a soft inquiry.

The CFPB also says requesting your own credit report does not hurt your score, and you can review your reports online for free once a week.

How Much Does a Hard Inquiry Lower Your Score?

Less than you might think. myFICO says that for most people, one additional hard inquiry results in the loss of less than five points from their FICO Score. The "new credit" category, which includes inquiries, makes up 10% of a FICO Score. Payment history (35%) and amounts owed (30%) matter much more; see how credit utilization works.

Experian says the effect on a VantageScore may be five to 10 points.

How Long Hard Inquiries Last

myFICO says hard inquiries typically remain on your credit report for up to two years, but FICO Scores only consider inquiries from the last 12 months. Experian says VantageScore can consider the prior 24 months. Either way, the effect fades over time and the inquiry drops off on its own.

FICO also ignores mortgage, auto and student loan inquiries made in the 30 days before your score is calculated, the CFPB says. But the grouping only works within one loan type: the CFPB notes that a mortgage inquiry and an auto loan inquiry count as more than one. Experian says rate shopping doesn't work for credit cards, so each card application is its own hard inquiry.

Tip: If you are shopping for a mortgage or auto loan, try to submit all your applications within 14 days so they are grouped under both FICO and VantageScore.

Rentals, Utilities and Phone Plans

Not every credit check is a hard inquiry. Experian lists requesting utilities or insurance as soft inquiries, and VantageScore says it excludes utility inquiries. A landlord's check for a lease can be a hard inquiry in some cases, Experian says. For landlords and phone carriers, myFICO says the inquiries generally don't affect FICO Scores, "but some of them might." If you're unsure, ask before you apply.

Bank account screening is a separate system. Experian says a ChexSystems report has no direct impact on your credit score.

What to Do About an Inquiry You Don't Recognize

  1. Check the name and date. Experian suggests contacting the company to verify the inquiry. A car dealer that sends one application to several lenders can create several legitimate inquiries.
  2. Dispute it if it's wrong. You can dispute a hard inquiry you believe is an error or the result of fraud, and disputing is free. Experian says inquiries can't be disputed through its online process, so call the number on your report. See how to dispute a credit report error.
  3. Freeze your credit if it's fraud. A freeze blocks new creditors from pulling your file, though the CFPB says creditors on accounts you already hold can still access it. Learn how to freeze your credit.

Stopping Prescreened Offers

Prescreened offers create soft inquiries, and the FTC says prescreening doesn't hurt your credit score. If you'd rather not get them, a credit freeze won't stop them, according to Equifax. Instead, you can opt out for five years at OptOutPrescreen.com or by calling 1-888-5-OPT-OUT (1-888-567-8688). TransUnion says a permanent opt-out requires mailing a signed form. The FTC says offers may take several weeks to stop.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many points does a hard inquiry take off your credit score?

For most people, one additional hard inquiry lowers a FICO Score by less than five points, according to myFICO. Experian says the effect on a VantageScore may be five to 10 points.

How long does a hard inquiry stay on your credit report?

Hard inquiries can stay on your credit report for up to two years. FICO Scores only consider inquiries from the last 12 months, while VantageScore can consider the prior 24 months.

Does checking my own credit score hurt it?

No. Checking your own credit report or score is a soft inquiry and has no effect on your score. You can review your credit reports online for free once a week at AnnualCreditReport.com.

Does shopping for a mortgage or car loan hurt my credit?

Less than you might expect. Newer FICO Scores count multiple mortgage, auto or student loan inquiries within 45 days as a single inquiry, older FICO versions use 14 days, and VantageScore uses a 14-day window. This does not apply to credit card applications.

Can I remove a hard inquiry from my credit report?

Only if it is an error or the result of fraud. You can dispute an inquiry you did not authorize with the credit bureau for free. Legitimate hard inquiries stay on your report for up to two years and drop off on their own.
