I Bonds: Current Rate (5.51%) and How to Buy

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 7 min read

General information, not financial advice. Rules from 31 CFR Part 359 and the IRS; current rate as reported by NerdWallet and Money.com.

Series I savings bonds bought from November 1, 2026 through April 30, 2027 earn a composite rate of 5.51% for their first six months, made up of a 1.40% fixed rate that lasts for the life of the bond and an inflation rate that resets every six months. You buy them electronically at TreasuryDirect.gov, up to $10,000 per person per calendar year, and you must hold them at least 12 months. Interest is subject to federal income tax but exempt from state and local income tax.

Key Takeaways

  • I bonds issued from November 1, 2026 through April 30, 2027 earn a 5.51% composite rate: a 1.40% fixed rate plus a 2.04% semiannual inflation rate (NerdWallet, Tipswatch).
  • The rate for May through October 2026 was 4.26%, with a 0.90% fixed rate (Quartz).
  • You can buy up to $10,000 of electronic I bonds per calendar year, in any amount from $25 to the penny (31 CFR 363.52 and 363.53).
  • I bonds can't be cashed for 12 months, and cashing before five years costs the last three months of interest (31 CFR 359.6 and 359.7).
  • Since January 1, 2025, you can no longer buy paper I bonds with your federal tax refund (IRS).

Wolf Street reported that the 1.40% fixed rate is the highest since 2006, up from 0.90% in the prior six months. The fixed rate matters most if you plan to hold for years, because it never changes. The inflation part changes every six months.

To put 5.51% in dollars: the rate is annualized, so a $10,000 bond earns about half of 5.51%, or roughly $275, over its first six months. After that, the bond earns whatever new inflation rate is announced, plus its 1.40% fixed rate.

How the I Bond Rate Works

An I bond's rate has two parts, set by federal regulation (31 CFR Part 359):

  • Fixed rate. Set when you buy. The regulation says it "is established for the life of the bond."
  • Semiannual inflation rate. Based on changes in the non-seasonally adjusted Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U). The Treasury announces new rates each May 1 and November 1.

The two combine into the composite rate. The formula in 31 CFR 359.14 is: composite rate = [(fixed rate ÷ 2) + semiannual inflation rate + (semiannual inflation rate × fixed rate ÷ 2)] × 2. With the current numbers: [(0.70% + 2.04% + 2.04% × 0.007)] × 2 = 5.51%.

The inflation part of your bond's rate resets every six months. Interest compounds semiannually. The composite rate can never go below 0%, so the bond's value never falls, even if inflation turns negative. An I bond earns interest for up to 30 years.

Before 2025, Kiplinger notes, you could buy up to $5,000 more in paper I bonds each year with your federal tax refund. The IRS says that option ended on January 1, 2025, and Form 8888 now states that buying savings bonds with your refund has been discontinued.

The 12-month lockup is the main trade-off. Unlike a high-yield savings account, you can't touch the money for a year, so I bonds don't suit money you may need within a year.

How I Bonds Are Taxed

  • Federal income tax: yes. Interest is subject to federal income tax.
  • State and local income tax: no. The bond terms say I bonds are exempt from state and local taxation, though federal and state estate, inheritance and gift taxes still apply.
  • Tax deferral. Experian notes you won't owe tax on the interest until you redeem the bond (or it matures).
  • Education exclusion. If you use the proceeds for qualified tuition and fees, the interest may be tax-free under the Education Savings Bond Program (IRS Form 8815). For 2025, the exclusion phases out between $99,500 and $114,500 of modified adjusted gross income for single filers and between $149,250 and $179,250 for married couples filing jointly. The bond owner must have been at least 24 before the bond was issued, and you can't use the married filing separately status.

If you're saving for college, compare I bonds with a 529 plan, which has much higher contribution limits.

How to Buy I Bonds, Step by Step

  1. Open a TreasuryDirect account. Go to TreasuryDirect.gov, choose to open an account, and pick an individual TreasuryDirect account. Under 31 CFR 363.11, you need a Social Security number, must be at least 18, need a U.S. address of record, and need an account at a U.S. bank or credit union.
  2. Link your bank account. You enter your bank's routing number and your account number. Purchases are paid by debit from this account.
  3. Choose BuyDirect. Select Series I and enter an amount from $25 to $10,000. You can also schedule recurring purchases.
  4. Hold for at least 12 months. Your bonds are held electronically in your TreasuryDirect account. You can redeem them after 12 months, keeping in mind the three-month interest penalty before five years.

Buying for others. A parent can open a minor's account linked to their own primary account (31 CFR 363.27). You can buy I bonds as gifts, which count toward the recipient's $10,000 limit when delivered. Trusts and entities such as corporations, partnerships and estates can also own electronic I bonds through their own accounts (31 CFR 363.20).

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the current I bond rate?

I bonds bought from November 1, 2026 through April 30, 2027 earn a 5.51% composite rate for their first six months. That includes a 1.40% fixed rate that lasts for the life of the bond and a 2.04% semiannual inflation rate.

How much can I invest in I bonds per year?

You can buy up to $10,000 in electronic I bonds per person per calendar year through TreasuryDirect. Gift bonds count toward the recipient's limit in the year they are delivered. Paper I bonds through tax refunds ended on January 1, 2025.

Can I cash out I bonds early?

Not in the first 12 months. After that you can redeem at any time, but if you cash in before five years you lose the last three months of interest.

Are I bonds taxed?

Interest is subject to federal income tax but exempt from state and local income taxes. You can defer federal tax until you redeem the bond, and the interest may be tax-free if used for qualified higher education expenses and you meet the income limits.

When do I bond rates change?

The Treasury announces new rates each May 1 and November 1. Your bond's fixed rate never changes, but its inflation rate resets every six months.
