Credit Utilization: How It Affects Your Credit Score

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 7 min read

Based on myFICO, VantageScore, CFPB and Experian, checked November 2026.

Credit utilization is the share of your available revolving credit that you are using: your credit card balances divided by your credit limits. It is part of the "amounts owed" category, which makes up 30% of a FICO Score, so keeping it low is one of the fastest ways to help your score. The CFPB notes that many experts recommend using less than 30% of your total limit, and people with the highest scores tend to stay in the single digits.

Key Takeaways

  • Credit utilization is your revolving balances divided by your revolving credit limits, multiplied by 100; for example, $2,000 owed on $10,000 of limits is 20%.
  • Amounts owed, which includes credit utilization, determines 30% of a FICO Score, according to myFICO.
  • myFICO says people with 850 FICO Scores have an average overall utilization rate of around 4.1%.
  • Card issuers generally report your balance to the credit bureaus at the end of each statement period, so paying before the statement closes can lower the utilization the bureaus see.
  • Only revolving accounts such as credit cards and credit lines count toward utilization; installment loans like auto loans and mortgages are scored separately.

What Is Credit Utilization?

Experian defines credit utilization as "the percentage of your available revolving credit that you're currently using." Revolving credit is credit you can borrow, repay and borrow again, such as credit cards and lines of credit. Your credit limit is the most you can borrow on each account.

Scoring models look at two versions of the number. myFICO says FICO Scores consider your overall utilization rate and the highest utilization rates on specific revolving accounts. So one maxed-out card can hurt even when your total ratio looks fine.

How to Calculate Your Credit Utilization Ratio

  1. Add up your revolving balances. Use the balances on all your credit cards and credit lines.
  2. Add up your credit limits. Use the limits on the same accounts.
  3. Divide and multiply by 100. Total balances divided by total limits, times 100, is your overall utilization rate.
  4. Repeat for each card. Run the same math on every card to spot one that is close to its limit.

How Much Utilization Counts in Your Score

In FICO Scores, myFICO says "amounts owed" determines 30% of the score. Utilization is a major part of that category, alongside things like how much you still owe on installment loans. The other FICO categories are payment history (35%), length of credit history (15%), new credit (10%) and credit mix (10%).

VantageScore weighs it in a similar way. Its own guide to VantageScore 4.0 lists credit utilization at 20%, next to payment history at 41% and depth of credit at 20%. Balances (6%) and available credit (2%) are listed as separate, smaller factors.

What Is a Good Credit Utilization Ratio?

There is no single cutoff. The CFPB says "many experts recommend using less than 30 percent of your total credit limit," and VantageScore gives the same 30% guideline. Experian says 30% is the point where utilization starts to have a more pronounced negative effect, but also that there is no specific point where it goes from good to bad.

Lower is generally better. Experian says people with excellent scores tend to have ratios below 10%, VantageScore points to the single digits for an excellent score, and myFICO says people with 850 FICO Scores average around 4.1%. myFICO also cautions that the 30% point alone "doesn't determine who has good or bad credit."

Zero is not the target. The guidance above is about keeping reported balances low compared with your limits. You can still use your cards; the point is to keep the balance that gets reported small and to pay on time.

When Your Balance Gets Reported

Your score uses the balances on your credit report at the moment the score is calculated. Experian says card issuers generally report your balance to the bureaus at the end of each statement period. That means you can carry a high balance during the month, pay it in full by the due date, and still show high utilization if the statement balance was large.

Experian notes you may be able to lower the reported balance by paying before your statement period ends. Many models don't look beyond the most recently reported balances and limits, so a lower balance can help as soon as it is reported. Newer models are different: Experian says VantageScore 4.0 and FICO Score 10 T consider trended data, and FICO says 10 T looks at balances over the previous 24 or more months.

Ways to Lower Your Credit Utilization

  1. Pay down card balances. This lowers the top of the ratio directly. If you are carrying debt on several cards, see our debt payoff calculator.
  2. Pay before the statement closes. An extra payment before the closing date can shrink the balance that gets reported.
  3. Ask for a higher limit. Experian suggests asking issuers to raise limits, but warns a request can sometimes lead to a hard inquiry. Read more in hard vs. soft credit inquiries.
  4. Think before closing old cards. Experian says closing a card can raise your utilization because it cuts your available credit. myFICO says a closed account stops counting once its balance is reported as $0.
  5. Spread out spending. Because FICO also looks at your highest per-card rate, keeping any single card from running near its limit can help.

What Counts and What Doesn't

Experian says only revolving accounts are included in utilization, including cards where you are an authorized user. Installment loans, such as auto loans, student loans and mortgages, are treated separately: myFICO says scores look at how much of the original loan amount you still owe. Experian notes that moving card debt to a personal loan shifts it from revolving to installment debt.

If you are an authorized user, myFICO says those accounts have less impact than your own accounts in recent FICO versions, but a primary cardholder's high utilization could still hurt your score.

The Bigger Picture

Americans carried $1.285 trillion in credit card balances in the third quarter of 2026, up $22 billion from the prior quarter, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. If you are among them, lowering your reported balances can help your score as soon as the new balances are reported. Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good credit utilization ratio?

Many experts recommend using less than 30% of your total credit limit, according to the CFPB. Lower is generally better: Experian says people with excellent scores tend to stay below 10%, and myFICO says people with 850 FICO Scores average around 4.1%.

How do I calculate my credit utilization?

Add up the balances on your credit cards and other revolving accounts, divide by the total of their credit limits, and multiply by 100. For example, $2,000 in balances on $10,000 of limits is 20%. Run the same math on each card too, because scores also look at per-card utilization.

Does paying my card in full every month mean my utilization is zero?

Not necessarily. Issuers generally report the balance at the end of each statement period, so if you pay after the statement closes, the reported balance can still be high. Paying before the statement closing date can lower the balance the bureaus see.

Do installment loans count toward credit utilization?

No. Utilization only includes revolving accounts such as credit cards and lines of credit. Auto loans, student loans and mortgages are scored separately, based partly on how much of the original amount you still owe.

Will closing a credit card hurt my utilization?

It can. Closing a card removes its credit limit from your total available credit, which can raise your utilization ratio if you carry balances on other cards. A closed card stops counting once its balance is reported as $0.
