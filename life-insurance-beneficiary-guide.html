How to Choose a Life Insurance Beneficiary

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 8 min read

General information, not legal advice. Based on the NAIC, OPM, SSA and insurer guidance, checked November 2026.

To choose a life insurance beneficiary, name at least one primary beneficiary (who gets paid first) and one contingent beneficiary (who gets paid if every primary beneficiary has died), using full legal names and percentage shares that add up to 100%. If you name no one, or everyone you named has died, the proceeds typically go to your estate and through probate. Your beneficiary form generally controls the payout, not your will, so review it after a marriage, divorce, birth or death.

Key Takeaways

  • A primary beneficiary is first in line for the death benefit; a contingent beneficiary is paid only if every primary beneficiary has died.
  • If no beneficiary is named or all named beneficiaries have died, the insurer typically pays your estate, so the money can go through probate and may be subject to estate taxes, according to the NAIC.
  • Most insurance companies will not pay life insurance proceeds directly to a minor, the NAIC says, so naming a trust or an adult custodian can avoid a court-appointed guardian.
  • A beneficiary designation generally controls who gets the death benefit even if your will says something different, unless the proceeds are payable to your estate.
  • Leaving money directly to someone on SSI can affect eligibility, because SSI countable resources cannot be worth more than $2,000 for an individual or $3,000 for a couple.

Who Can Be a Beneficiary?

A beneficiary is the person or entity that receives the death benefit when the insured person dies. Guardian Life notes that a beneficiary doesn't have to be a person: it can be a charity, a church or a family trust. You can also leave the proceeds to your estate, where your executor distributes them after probate (the court process that settles an estate).

Primary vs. Contingent Beneficiaries

Your primary beneficiary is first in line. Your contingent (or secondary) beneficiary is next in line and is paid only if every primary beneficiary has died. Naming both is the simplest way to keep the money out of your estate.

You can split the benefit among several people. MetLife's group beneficiary form, for example, asks for each person's name, date of birth, Social Security number, contact details and relationship to you, and requires the primary shares to total 100% and the contingent shares to total 100%, in whole numbers. On that form, if one primary beneficiary dies before you, their share is split equally among the remaining primaries.

Per Stirpes vs. Per Capita

These Latin terms decide what happens to a share when a beneficiary dies before you. Guardian Life explains them this way:

  • Per stirpes ("by branch"): if a primary beneficiary dies before you, that person's share is divided equally among his or her heirs. Example: you name your two children 50/50; one dies, and that child's 50% goes to their children.
  • Per capita ("by head"): the proceeds are divided equally among the beneficiaries who are still alive. In the same example, your surviving child would get 100%.

Check your policy's beneficiary form to see which rule applies to your designation.

Revocable vs. Irrevocable Beneficiaries

Most designations are revocable, meaning you can change them later. An irrevocable beneficiary has legal rights to the policy, and you can't change the designation without their written consent, according to Western & Southern and Guardian Life.

Naming a Minor Child

The NAIC says most insurance companies will not pay life insurance proceeds to minors. New York Life explains that state law then requires a guardian to be appointed to manage the money, which can mean a court process. Nolo adds that without a plan, a child may receive the money outright at 18.

Two common alternatives:

  • A custodian under your state's Uniform Transfers to Minors Act (UTMA). A custodian you name manages the money until the child reaches the age set by state law, usually between 18 and 21, according to New York Life.
  • A trust. You set the terms, such as paying for education first or releasing money at a later age. The trust is named as the beneficiary.

What Happens if There's No Beneficiary?

If you never named a beneficiary, or everyone you named has died, Guardian Life says the insurer will default to paying your estate. The NAIC says the proceeds then typically go through probate with the rest of your assets and might be subject to estate taxes. Nolo notes the money also reaches your family more slowly and may be used to pay your debts.

Some plans set their own order. For federal employees' FEGLI coverage, the Office of Personnel Management pays, in order: your widow or widower; your children in equal shares (with a deceased child's share going to that child's descendants); your parents in equal shares; the executor or administrator of your estate; and then next of kin under your state's laws.

Your Beneficiary Form vs. Your Will

A beneficiary designation generally controls who gets the death benefit, even if your will says something different. The NAIC says your will does not control life insurance payouts unless the money goes to your estate. That is why an outdated form can send money to an ex-spouse.

  • Divorce. Some states have laws that revoke an ex-spouse's designation after divorce. In Sveen v. Melin (2018), the Supreme Court upheld applying Minnesota's law to a policy bought before the divorce. But for employer plans covered by the federal ERISA law, the Court in Egelhoff v. Egelhoff (2001) held that a Washington revocation statute was preempted, so the plan documents controlled. The safe move is to update the form yourself.
  • Community property states. Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin are community property states, per the IRS. Nolo advises that if you live in one and want to name someone other than your spouse, you get your spouse's written consent.

Beneficiaries Who Receive Government Benefits

Leaving money directly to someone who gets Supplemental Security Income (SSI) or Medicaid can affect their eligibility. The Social Security Administration says countable resources for SSI cannot be worth more than $2,000 for an individual or $3,000 for a couple. The National Council on Aging says a special needs trust can shelter an inheritance from being counted. SSA rules say a properly set up third-party trust, funded by someone else and which the beneficiary can't revoke or use for their own support, is not counted as the beneficiary's resource. Talk to an attorney who handles special needs planning before naming such a trust.

How to Name or Change a Beneficiary

  1. Get the form. Ask your insurer or, for work coverage, your benefits department. Federal employees use OPM's form SF 2823 for FEGLI.
  2. Use full legal names. The NAIC suggests a person's full name rather than "my husband" or "my wife." Add date of birth and Social Security number if the form asks.
  3. Set percentages. Make sure each level adds up to 100%.
  4. Name a contingent beneficiary. This keeps the money out of probate if a primary beneficiary dies first.
  5. Review regularly. The NAIC suggests checking every few years and after births, marriages, divorces or deaths. Keep a copy of the confirmation.

Coverage through your job has its own beneficiary form, separate from any individual policy; see life insurance through work. For taxes, see is life insurance taxable, and for what your beneficiaries will do later, how to file a life insurance claim.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a primary and contingent beneficiary?

The primary beneficiary is first in line to receive the death benefit. The contingent, or secondary, beneficiary is paid only if every primary beneficiary has died before you. Naming both helps keep the money out of your estate and probate.

What happens if a life insurance policy has no beneficiary?

If you never named a beneficiary or all of your beneficiaries have died, the insurer typically pays the death benefit to your estate. The money then generally goes through probate, can take longer to reach your family, and may be used to pay debts or be subject to estate taxes.

Can I name a minor child as my life insurance beneficiary?

You can, but most insurers will not pay proceeds directly to a minor. Without a plan, a court may need to appoint a guardian to manage the money. Naming a custodian under your state's Uniform Transfers to Minors Act or setting up a trust are common alternatives.

Does my will override my life insurance beneficiary?

Generally no. The beneficiary designation on the policy usually controls who receives the death benefit, even if your will says something different. Your will only controls the money if the proceeds are payable to your estate.

How often should I review my life insurance beneficiaries?

The NAIC suggests reviewing your beneficiaries every few years and after major life events, such as a marriage, divorce, birth, adoption or death in the family.
