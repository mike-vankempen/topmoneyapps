What Is a Stock Split? How Splits and Reverse Splits Work

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 7 min read

General information, not investment or tax advice, and not a recommendation to buy or sell any stock. Based on FINRA, SEC Investor.gov and IRS guidance, checked November 2026.

A stock split is when a company divides its existing shares into more shares at a lower price per share, so the total value of your position stays the same. In a 2-for-1 split, 10 shares at $200 become 20 shares at $100, still worth $2,000; a reverse split does the opposite, combining shares into fewer, higher-priced ones.

Key Takeaways

  • In a stock split, a company issues more shares to existing holders at a set ratio, and the share price falls in proportion, so your total position value and the company's market value do not change (FINRA).
  • FINRA lists 2-for-1, 3-for-2 and 3-for-1 as the most common forward split ratios.
  • The IRS says "stock splits don't create a taxable event"; you spread your original cost basis across the new shares.
  • A reverse split combines shares, such as 1-for-10, and is often used to lift a stock above an exchange's $1.00 minimum bid price.
  • Recent large splits include Nvidia's 10-for-1 (trading split-adjusted from June 10, 2024), Chipotle's 50-for-1 (June 26, 2024) and Netflix's 10-for-1 (November 17, 2025).

How a Stock Split Works

In a forward split, the company's board issues additional shares to everyone who already owns the stock, at a fixed ratio. The share price drops by the same ratio. You own more shares, each worth less, and the total is unchanged. So is the company's market capitalization, which is the share price times the number of shares.

Dividends per share are adjusted the same way. If a company paid $1 per share before a 2-for-1 split, the per-share dividend would be adjusted to $0.50, so your total dividend income is unchanged by the split itself, according to Schwab.

Why Companies Split Their Stock

The usual reason is to bring a high share price down to a level the company sees as more accessible. Walmart said its 3-for-1 split in February 2024 was meant to make it easier for its associates to buy shares, and Netflix said its 10-for-1 split in November 2025 aimed at a price more accessible to employees in its stock option program.

A split does not change the business, its earnings or what you own as a share of the company. Schwab notes that brokerage accounts that offer fractional shares have made price-lowering splits less necessary, because you can already buy a slice of a high-priced stock with a few dollars.

Key Dates: Announcement, Record and Ex-Date

  1. Announcement: the company discloses the split, typically in a press release and SEC filing such as a Form 8-K.
  2. Record date: the cutoff for which shareholders are entitled to the new shares. Nvidia, for example, gave nine extra shares for each share held by holders of record at the close on June 6, 2024.
  3. Distribution (payable) date: the new shares are delivered to accounts.
  4. Ex-date and split-adjusted trading: under FINRA Rule 11140, for distributions of 25% or more, which includes most splits, the ex-date is the first business day after the payable date. That is when the stock starts trading at the new price.

Investor.gov explains the related "due bill" for stock dividends: if you sell between the record date and the ex-date, you also sell the right to the extra shares, and your broker may require you to deliver them. Ask your broker how it handles trades in that window.

Reverse Stock Splits

A reverse split cuts the number of shares and raises the price by the same ratio. Investor.gov's example: in a 1-for-10 reverse split, 10,000 shares become 1,000 shares. FINRA's example of a 200-for-1 reverse split turns 5,000 shares at $0.10 ($500) into 25 shares at $20 (still $500).

Companies often do this to regain an exchange's minimum price. Nasdaq requires a minimum bid price of $1.00. Under rules the SEC approved on January 17, 2025, a Nasdaq company falls out of compliance after 30 consecutive business days below $1.00 and generally gets 180 days to fix it. A company that did a reverse split in the prior year and falls below $1 again gets no compliance period, and neither does one whose reverse splits total 250-to-1 or more over two years.

Caution: FINRA says reverse splits often involve low-priced, high-risk stocks, and Investor.gov warns that investors "may lose money as a result of fluctuations in trading prices following reverse stock splits." A reverse split does not fix the business problems that pushed the price down.

Fractional Shares and Cash in Lieu

Splits do not always divide evenly. If a reverse split leaves you with part of a share, the company's terms decide what happens. Some companies pay "cash in lieu," meaning cash instead of the fractional share, and Investor.gov notes some small holders get cashed out entirely. Brokers that support fractional shares may keep the fraction instead: Robinhood says a 1-for-3 reverse split on 10 shares typically leaves you with 3.33 shares, and when cash in lieu is paid it arrives about three to four weeks after the corporate action.

Taxes and Cost Basis After a Split

The IRS says "stock splits don't create a taxable event." You must reallocate your cost basis, meaning what you paid, across the new shares. The IRS example: 100 shares with a $1,500 total basis become 200 shares after a 2-for-1 split, each with a $7.50 basis. Your total basis stays $1,500. Basis is what your gain or loss is measured against when you sell, which then falls under capital gains tax rates. Keep your records, especially for shares you moved between brokers.

What Happens to Options

Options contracts are adjusted so their economic value is unchanged. E*TRADE's example: after a 5-for-1 split, two call contracts with a $500 strike become 10 contracts with a $100 strike. The number of contracts and the strike change; the total exposure does not.

Do Stock Splits Matter to You?

For a long-term investor, a split changes the number on your share count and the price you see, not what you own. Do not treat a split, forward or reverse, as a reason on its own to buy or sell. If you invest a set amount regularly, dollar-cost averaging and broad index funds make individual split events largely irrelevant.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a stock split good or bad for shareholders?

Neither by itself. A split changes the number of shares and the price per share in proportion, so the total value of your holding and the company's market value stay the same.

Do you pay taxes on a stock split?

No. The IRS says stock splits don't create a taxable event. You spread your original cost basis across the new shares, so the total basis stays the same.

What is a reverse stock split?

A reverse split combines shares into fewer, higher-priced shares, such as 1-for-10. Companies often use it to get the price back above an exchange's minimum bid price, such as Nasdaq's $1.00.

What happens if I sell before a stock split?

If you sell between the record date and the ex-date, you may also be selling your right to the new shares, and your broker may require you to deliver them. Ask your broker before trading in that window.

What happens to fractional shares in a split?

It depends on the company's terms and your broker. Some companies pay cash in lieu of fractional shares; brokers that support fractional shares may keep the fraction in your account.
