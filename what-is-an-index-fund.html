What Is an Index Fund? How It Works, Costs and Risks

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 7 min read

General information, not investment advice. Based on SEC investor guidance at Investor.gov.

An index fund is a type of mutual fund or exchange-traded fund (ETF) that tries to match the returns of a market index, such as the S&P 500, instead of trying to beat it. Because index funds usually follow a passive strategy and trade less often, they can cost less than actively managed funds, but the SEC warns that not all of them do. Below: how index funds work, what they cost, their risks and what to check before you buy one.

Key Takeaways

  • An index fund is a type of mutual fund or exchange-traded fund (ETF) that seeks to track the returns of a market index, according to the SEC's Investor.gov.
  • The S&P 500, the Russell 2000 and the Wilshire 5000 Total Market Index are examples of market indexes that index funds may track (Investor.gov).
  • You cannot invest in an index directly; an index fund is an indirect way to get its returns.
  • Index funds generally follow a passive style and trade less often, which can lower costs, but the SEC says not all index funds cost less than actively managed funds.
  • Index fund risks include the risks of the underlying securities, tracking error, lack of flexibility and underperformance of the index due to fees and trading costs.

How an Index Fund Works

A market index is a list of securities chosen by a set of rules, used to measure a slice of the market. You cannot buy an index itself. An index fund pools money from many investors and buys the securities in the index, or a portion of them, so the fund's returns follow the index's returns.

The SEC's Investor.gov names the S&P 500 Index, the Russell 2000 Index and the Wilshire 5000 Total Market Index as a few examples of indexes that index funds may track. Indexes are built in different ways:

Index typeHow holdings are weightedExample named by Investor.gov
Market-cap weightedBigger companies (higher market capitalization, meaning share price times shares outstanding) get a bigger weightS&P 500
Price weightedStocks with higher share prices get a bigger weightDow Jones Industrial Average

Funds also track their index in different ways. Investor.gov explains that some index funds buy all the securities in the index, while others buy only a sample of them. Some also use derivatives such as options or futures. A fund that holds only a sample may be less likely to match the index exactly.

Index Funds vs. Actively Managed Funds

Index funds have generally followed a passive style. A passive fund aims to maximize returns over the long run by not buying and selling very often. An actively managed fund tries to beat an index, usually by trading more often based on research.

TradingLess frequentMore frequent

Both mutual funds and ETFs can be passive or active, according to FINRA and Investor.gov. So "index fund" describes the strategy, while "mutual fund" or "ETF" describes how the fund is structured and traded. Our ETF vs. mutual fund guide explains that difference.

What Index Funds Cost

Every fund charges annual operating expenses, often called the expense ratio: a yearly fee taken as a percentage of your investment. Investor.gov says passive management can lower costs because the fund does not need research analysts to choose securities. It also warns that not all index funds have lower costs than actively managed funds, and that if two funds hold the same investments, the one with lower costs will generally produce higher returns.

Risks of Index Funds

  • Market risk: an index fund carries the same general risks as the securities in its index. If the index falls, the fund falls.
  • Lack of flexibility: the fund follows its index rather than a manager's judgment, so it has little room to react when markets change.
  • Tracking error: a fund may not perfectly track its index, for example when it holds only a sample of the securities.
  • Underperformance: fees and expenses, trading costs and tracking error can leave the fund's return below the index's return.

"Smart Beta" and Other Non-Traditional Index Funds

Some funds track custom-built indexes instead of broad market indexes. Investor.gov groups these as non-traditional index funds, including smart beta funds (which select holdings by factors such as value, dividends or quality), quant funds (which use numerical models) and ESG funds (environmental, social and governance factors). The SEC cautions that these funds can be complex, will not necessarily match or beat the market, may have short track records and typically have higher expenses than traditional index funds.

Check the label: a fund with "index" in its name may track a narrow or custom index. Read how the index is built and look at the actual holdings before you invest.

How to Choose an Index Fund

Investor.gov suggests reading a fund's prospectus and most recent shareholder report, and asking these questions before you buy:

  1. What fees and expenses will I pay to buy, own and sell this fund?
  2. What specific risks does the fund carry?
  3. How is the fund's index put together?
  4. How does the fund's strategy fit my goals?

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an index fund in simple terms?

An index fund is a mutual fund or ETF that tries to match the returns of a market index, such as the S&P 500, by holding the securities in that index or a sample of them.

Are index funds cheaper than actively managed funds?

Often, because passive funds do not need analysts to pick securities and trade less often. But the SEC warns that not all index funds have lower costs than actively managed funds, so always check the fund's actual fees.

Can an index fund lose money?

Yes. An index fund carries the same general risks as the securities in the index it tracks. If the index falls, the fund's value falls too.

Is an index fund a mutual fund or an ETF?

It can be either. An index fund is a type of mutual fund or exchange-traded fund. The index strategy is the same; the difference is how the shares are bought, sold and priced.

What is tracking error?

Tracking error is the gap between a fund's return and its index's return. It can come from fees, trading costs, or holding only a sample of the index's securities.
