Roth 401(k) vs. Traditional 401(k): Taxes, Limits and Rules

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 8 min read

General information, not tax or investment advice. Plan features vary; check your plan documents and the IRS Roth comparison chart.

The difference is when you pay tax: a traditional 401(k) takes before-tax money and taxes your withdrawals, while a Roth 401(k) takes after-tax money and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. Both share one limit, $24,500 for 2026 and $25,500 for 2027, and neither has an income limit.

Key Takeaways

  • Traditional 401(k) contributions are made with before-tax dollars and withdrawals are taxed; Roth 401(k) contributions are after-tax and qualified withdrawals are tax-free (IRS Roth comparison chart).
  • Roth and traditional 401(k) deferrals share one limit: $24,500 for 2026 and $25,500 for 2027, plus catch-up contributions of $8,000 (2026) and $8,500 (2027) at age 50 and over.
  • A Roth 401(k) has no income limit, and since 2024 Roth 401(k) accounts have no required minimum distributions while the owner is alive (IRS).
  • Starting in 2026, catch-up contributions must be Roth for workers whose prior-year wages from the plan sponsor exceeded $150,000; the threshold rises to $155,000 for 2027 (IRS).
  • At year-end 2025, 98% of Vanguard plans offered a Roth option, but an estimated 18% of participants used it (Vanguard How America Saves 2026).

How the Taxes Work

The IRS's Roth comparison chart puts it simply: traditional 401(k) contributions are made with before-tax dollars, and withdrawals are subject to federal and most state income taxes. Roth 401(k) contributions are made with after-tax dollars, and qualified withdrawals, including the growth, are tax-free.

A Roth withdrawal is "qualified" only if both of these are true, according to the IRS:

  • It comes after a 5-taxable-year period that starts on the first day of the tax year in which you first made Roth contributions to that plan, and
  • It is made on or after age 59½, after your death, or because you are disabled.

If you take money out early, the earnings portion of a non-qualified Roth withdrawal is taxable, and IRS Topic 558 says a 10% additional tax generally applies to 401(k) distributions before age 59½ unless an exception applies.

A simple way to see the trade-off: for the same amount of after-tax money going in, the two accounts come out even if your tax rate is the same when you contribute and when you withdraw. The traditional 401(k) comes out ahead if your tax rate is lower in retirement, and the Roth comes out ahead if it is higher. Nobody knows future tax rates for sure, which is one reason some savers split contributions between the two. Our 2026 tax brackets page shows today's rates.

2026 and 2027 Contribution Limits

Roth and traditional 401(k) contributions share one limit. The IRS says total contributions to the pre-tax and Roth accounts cannot exceed the annual limit, and the limit applies per person, not per plan. If you have two jobs with two 401(k)s, you still get one limit.

Rules That Differ (and Rules That Changed)

No income limits

Unlike a Roth IRA, a Roth 401(k) is not subject to income limits. The IRS says designated Roth accounts are not subject to the modified gross income limits that restrict some people from contributing to Roth IRAs. That makes a Roth 401(k) a direct route to Roth savings for higher earners whose plan offers it. Compare the IRA version in our Roth IRA vs. traditional IRA guide.

No RMDs on Roth 401(k)s

Starting in 2024, designated Roth accounts in 401(k) and 403(b) plans are no longer subject to required minimum distributions while the owner is alive, according to the IRS. Traditional 401(k)s still require them; see our RMD guide. Beneficiaries who inherit either account still have to take RMDs.

Employer money can now be Roth, if your plan allows

Matching and other employer contributions have traditionally gone in pre-tax. Under IRS Notice 2024-2, plans may, but are not required to, let you choose Roth treatment for employer contributions made after December 29, 2022. A match can be Roth only if you are fully vested in it. Roth employer contributions count as income to you in the year they are put in your account, but they are not subject to income tax withholding, so you may need to adjust your withholding or set money aside.

High earners' catch-up contributions must be Roth

Starting in 2026, if your wages from the employer sponsoring the plan were more than $150,000 in the prior year, your catch-up contributions must go in as Roth, according to the IRS. For 2027, IRS Notice 2026-66 raises that threshold to $155,000 of 2026 wages. Final regulations generally apply to taxable years beginning after December 31, 2026, with a reasonable, good-faith standard before then.

Converting inside the plan

If your plan allows it, an in-plan Roth rollover lets you move vested traditional money into the Roth account. The IRS says you generally include the taxable amount in income, and you cannot reverse (recharacterize) it.

Rollovers when you leave a job

A Roth 401(k) can roll into a Roth IRA, but not into a traditional IRA. One catch: the IRS says years in the Roth 401(k) do not count toward the Roth IRA's own 5-year period for qualified distributions, though an existing Roth IRA's earlier start date applies. How People Choose

The Roth option is now nearly universal but not widely used. In Vanguard's How America Saves 2026 report, 98% of Vanguard plans offered a Roth feature at year-end 2025, while an estimated 18% of participants in those plans had elected it.

These are the factors that usually decide it, based on the IRS rules above:

  • Your tax rate now vs. later. A lower expected rate in retirement favors traditional; a higher one favors Roth.
  • Cash flow today. Traditional contributions reduce this year's taxable income, so the same contribution costs less from your paycheck.
  • Tax diversification. Having both pre-tax and Roth money gives you a choice of which to tap in retirement.
  • RMDs. Roth 401(k) money is not subject to lifetime RMDs; traditional money is.
  • The match. Whatever you choose, check whether contributing enough to get your full employer match is possible; it is part of the same plan either way.

This is a general comparison, not advice for your situation. A tax professional can run the numbers with your actual income and plans. If you are also deciding where an IRA fits, see 401(k) vs. Roth IRA, and use our open enrollment checklist when you update your elections.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a Roth 401(k) and a traditional 401(k)?

A traditional 401(k) uses before-tax money, lowering your taxable income now, and withdrawals are taxed. A Roth 401(k) uses after-tax money, and qualified withdrawals, including growth, are tax-free. Both share the same contribution limit and neither has an income limit.

Can I contribute to both a Roth 401(k) and a traditional 401(k)?

Yes, if your plan offers both, but the total of your Roth and traditional deferrals cannot exceed the annual limit: $24,500 for 2026 and $25,500 for 2027, plus any catch-up contributions you qualify for.

Is there an income limit for a Roth 401(k)?

No. The IRS says designated Roth accounts are not subject to the income limits that restrict Roth IRA contributions.

Do Roth 401(k)s have required minimum distributions?

Not while you are alive, starting in 2024. Traditional 401(k)s still require RMDs. Beneficiaries who inherit a Roth 401(k) still have to take distributions.

When is a Roth 401(k) withdrawal tax-free?

When it is a qualified distribution: it comes after a 5-taxable-year period that starts with your first Roth contribution to the plan, and you are 59 and a half or older, disabled, or it is paid after your death.

Do high earners have to make Roth catch-up contributions?

Yes. Starting in 2026, if your prior-year wages from the employer sponsoring the plan were over $150,000, your catch-up contributions must be Roth. For 2027 the threshold is $155,000 of 2026 wages.
