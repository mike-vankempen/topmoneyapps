FDIC Insurance: What It Covers and the $250,000 Limit

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 7 min read

General information, not legal or financial advice. Based on the FDIC's Understanding Deposit Insurance page and its "Your Insured Deposits" guide, checked November 2026.

FDIC insurance protects deposits at FDIC-insured banks up to at least $250,000 per depositor, per bank, for each ownership category. If your bank fails, insured money in checking, savings, money market deposit accounts and CDs is paid back by the FDIC; investments such as stocks, mutual funds and crypto are not covered, even if you bought them through a bank.

Key Takeaways

  • The FDIC insures deposits up to at least $250,000 per depositor, per ownership category, at each FDIC-insured bank.
  • There are seven FDIC ownership categories, including single, joint, certain retirement and trust accounts, and each is insured separately.
  • Trust accounts are insured up to $250,000 per eligible beneficiary, with a cap of $1,250,000 per owner per bank under the rule in effect since April 1, 2024.
  • Stocks, bonds, mutual funds, annuities, life insurance, crypto assets, Treasury securities and safe deposit box contents are not FDIC-insured.
  • The FDIC says that since it was founded in 1933, "no depositor has lost a penny of FDIC-insured funds."

How the $250,000 Limit Works

The FDIC, or Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, is the federal agency that insures bank deposits. Its coverage is backed by the full faith and credit of the U.S. government. The standard limit is $250,000 per depositor, per FDIC-insured bank, per ownership category.

Three parts of that rule matter:

  • Per depositor: the limit applies to each person, not each account. Two savings accounts in your name at the same bank are added together.
  • Per bank: money at a different FDIC-insured bank gets its own $250,000 of coverage.
  • Per ownership category: accounts held in different legal ways, such as a single account and a joint account, are insured separately at the same bank.

So you can have more than $250,000 insured at one bank if the money sits in different ownership categories. The FDIC's online tool, EDIE (Electronic Deposit Insurance Estimator), calculates your coverage account by account.

The Seven Ownership Categories

The FDIC lists seven ownership categories. The ones most households use are the first four.

Example: a married couple at one bank

Say you and your spouse each have a single savings account and you share a joint checking account. Each of you is insured up to $250,000 in your single account. In the joint category, each of you is insured up to $250,000 for your share. Together that is up to $1,000,000 at one bank: $250,000 plus $250,000 in single accounts and $500,000 in the joint account. If you each also have an IRA deposit at the bank, those sit in the separate retirement category. Confirm your own setup with EDIE, because the bank's records decide how each account is titled.

The Trust Account Rule Since April 1, 2024

The FDIC simplified trust coverage on April 1, 2024. Revocable trusts (including payable-on-death accounts) and irrevocable trusts now follow one rule: $250,000 per eligible beneficiary, up to $1,250,000 per trust owner per bank. You reach the cap with five or more beneficiaries. Beneficiaries must be living people or qualifying charities or nonprofits. The rule applies to existing and new trust accounts, including CDs, whatever their purchase or maturity date. What Happens If Your Bank Fails

When an insured bank fails, another bank may take over its deposits. When that does not happen and the FDIC pays depositors directly, the FDIC says "such payments usually begin within a few days after the bank's closing."

Failures are rare. The FDIC's 2025 failure list shows one bank: Pulaski Savings Bank in Chicago, in January 2025. In 2026, news outlets counted six failures by late September; Yahoo Finance reported Nano Banc of Irvine, California, as the sixth on September 28, 2026.

Above the limit? Money over the insurance limit is not guaranteed. Spread large balances across ownership categories or across separate FDIC-insured banks, and check whether a bank is insured with the FDIC's BankFind tool.

Fintech Apps and Pass-Through Coverage

Many apps that offer savings or checking are not banks. They place your money at partner banks, often in pooled "custodial" accounts. In September 2024 the FDIC proposed a recordkeeping rule for these accounts that would require banks to keep records of each owner's balance and ownership category; as of November 2026 we found no final version of that rule. Before you deposit, read the app's disclosures to see which bank holds your funds and how it says FDIC coverage applies. Compare options in our high-yield savings comparison.

Is the $250,000 Limit Changing?

Not as of November 2026. In March 2026, senators introduced the Main Street Depositor Protection Act (S. 4198), which would let the FDIC raise coverage, to between $250,000 and $5 million, only for noninterest-bearing transaction accounts such as business payroll accounts. Government records show it was introduced and referred to the Senate Banking Committee; we found no record of it becoming law. The standard limit for everyday deposits remains $250,000.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the FDIC insurance limit?

The standard limit is $250,000 per depositor, per FDIC-insured bank, for each ownership category. Accounts in different ownership categories, such as single and joint accounts, are insured separately.

How much is a joint account insured for?

Each co-owner is insured up to $250,000 for their share of all joint accounts at the same bank, so a joint account owned by two people can be insured up to $500,000.

Is my money insured above $250,000?

Only if it sits in different ownership categories or at different FDIC-insured banks. Money above the limit in one category at one bank is not guaranteed.

Are stocks and mutual funds FDIC-insured?

No. Stocks, bonds, mutual funds, annuities, life insurance, crypto assets and Treasury securities are not FDIC-insured, even if you buy them through a bank. SIPC protects brokerage customers if the firm fails, but not against market losses.

How fast do you get your money back if a bank fails?

When the FDIC pays depositors directly, it says payments usually begin within a few days after the bank closes. In other cases another bank takes over the deposits.

Are credit unions FDIC-insured?

No. Federally insured credit unions are covered by the NCUA's share insurance fund, which also insures up to $250,000 and is backed by the full faith and credit of the United States.
