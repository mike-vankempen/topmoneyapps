Backdoor Roth IRA Explained: Steps, Taxes and the Pro-Rata Rule

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 7 min read

General information, not tax or investment advice. Confirm details with the IRS Form 8606 instructions or a tax professional before converting.

A backdoor Roth IRA is a way to get money into a Roth IRA when your income is too high to contribute directly: you make a nondeductible contribution to a traditional IRA, then convert it to a Roth IRA. It is not a separate type of account. For 2026, direct Roth contributions are off-limits at modified AGI of $168,000 or more for single filers and $252,000 or more for married couples filing jointly. The catch is the pro-rata rule: if you hold other pre-tax IRA money, part of your conversion is taxable.

Key Takeaways

  • A backdoor Roth IRA is not a separate account type; it is a nondeductible traditional IRA contribution followed by a conversion to a Roth IRA (Fidelity).
  • For 2026 you cannot contribute directly to a Roth IRA if your modified AGI is $168,000 or more (single) or $252,000 or more (married filing jointly), per Fidelity and Schwab.
  • The 2026 IRA contribution limit is $7,500, plus a $1,100 catch-up if you are 50 or older, according to Fidelity and Schwab.
  • The pro-rata rule counts all your pre-tax traditional, rollover, SEP and SIMPLE IRA money, so a conversion is taxable in proportion to that pre-tax share (Schwab).
  • You must file IRS Form 8606 with your tax return to report nondeductible contributions and Roth conversions.

Who a Backdoor Roth Is For

A Roth IRA is funded with after-tax money in exchange for tax-free qualified withdrawals later. (Our Roth IRA vs. traditional IRA guide covers the basics.) The problem for higher earners is that direct Roth contributions phase out as income rises. The backdoor route, as Fidelity and Schwab describe it, goes around that limit in two steps: put after-tax money into a traditional IRA, then convert it to a Roth IRA.

If your income sits in the partial range, you can contribute part of the limit directly and still use the backdoor for the rest. If your income is below the range, you do not need a backdoor at all. See all the 2026 figures on our 2026 contribution limits page.

How to Do a Backdoor Roth IRA: 3 Steps

  1. Make a nondeductible traditional IRA contribution. Open a traditional IRA (or use one you have) and contribute after-tax money, up to $7,500 for 2026 ($8,600 if you are 50 or older). Because your income is high, you usually cannot deduct it, which is what makes it "nondeductible."
  2. Convert it to a Roth IRA. Open a Roth IRA and move the money over as a conversion. Most brokerages let you do this online.
  3. Report it on Form 8606. The IRS requires Form 8606 when you make nondeductible traditional IRA contributions and when you convert traditional IRA money to a Roth. You file it with your Form 1040 by the return's due date, including extensions. Schwab advises filing Form 8606 every year you do a backdoor Roth.

If the money earns anything between the contribution and the conversion, that growth is taxable when you convert, Fidelity notes. Fidelity also says a conversion must be completed by December 31 to count in that year's taxable income.

Timing note: the IRS instructions for Form 8606 gave April 15, 2026 as the deadline for 2025 IRA contributions, so contributions for a tax year can be made into the next spring. The conversion is reported in the calendar year it actually happens. Check when taxes are due in 2027 for this year's dates.

The Pro-Rata Rule: The Biggest Trap

The pro-rata rule decides how much of a conversion is taxed. You cannot choose to convert only your after-tax dollars. Fidelity explains that the IRS views all of your traditional IRAs as a single tax entity (Roth and inherited IRAs are excluded), and Schwab says the rule applies to all pre-tax IRA assets, including traditional, rollover, SEP and SIMPLE IRAs. The taxable share of any conversion matches the pre-tax share of that combined balance.

Example (Schwab)Amount
New after-tax (nondeductible) contribution$7,000
Existing pre-tax IRA money$93,000
Total IRA balance$100,000 (93% pre-tax)
Convert $7,000: taxable part$6,510 (93%)
Convert $7,000: tax-free part$490 (7%)

Fidelity gives a similar example: with $50,000 across traditional IRAs that is 10% nondeductible and 90% deductible contributions and earnings, converting $5,000 makes $4,500 (90%) taxable.

The takeaway: a backdoor Roth is cleanest when you have no other pre-tax IRA money. If you do, run the numbers before you convert, because the tax bill can be much larger than you expect. A rollover IRA from an old 401(k) counts too, so think about that before rolling over a 401(k) into an IRA.

Other Rules to Know

  • 5-year rule for conversions: each conversion has its own 5-year clock. Withdrawing converted money before age 59½ and before that conversion's 5 years are up can trigger a penalty, according to Schwab and Fidelity.
  • No undo: the IRS does not allow recharacterization (reversal) of Roth conversions made in 2018 or later.
  • Step-transaction risk: Schwab notes the IRS has not issued formal guidance on whether a backdoor Roth violates the "step-transaction" doctrine, which treats a series of steps as one transaction. Schwab describes this as carrying some risk.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a backdoor Roth IRA allowed?

It is a widely used strategy described by major brokerages such as Fidelity and Schwab: a nondeductible traditional IRA contribution followed by a Roth conversion. Schwab notes the IRS has not issued formal guidance on whether it violates the step-transaction rule, so it carries some risk.

What are the 2026 income limits that make a backdoor Roth useful?

For 2026 you cannot contribute directly to a Roth IRA if your modified AGI is $168,000 or more as a single filer or $252,000 or more if married filing jointly. Partial contributions are allowed from $153,000 and $242,000.

What is the pro-rata rule?

The pro-rata rule makes a Roth conversion taxable in proportion to the pre-tax money in all your traditional, rollover, SEP and SIMPLE IRAs combined. In Schwab's example, $7,000 after-tax plus $93,000 pre-tax means 93% of a $7,000 conversion, or $6,510, is taxable.

Do I need to file Form 8606 for a backdoor Roth?

Yes. The IRS requires Form 8606 when you make nondeductible traditional IRA contributions and when you convert traditional IRA money to a Roth IRA. It is filed with your Form 1040.

Can I undo a backdoor Roth conversion?

No. The IRS does not allow recharacterization of Roth conversions made in 2018 or later.
