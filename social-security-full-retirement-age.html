Social Security Full Retirement Age by Birth Year

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 7 min read

General information, not financial advice. Ages and percentages are from the Social Security Administration, including 20 CFR 404.409 and its Retirement Age and Benefit Reduction page.

Your full retirement age for Social Security is 67 if you were born in 1960 or later and 66 if you were born from 1943 through 1954. For births from 1955 through 1959, it rises by two months per year. Full retirement age is when you get 100% of your benefit: claiming at 62 cuts it by up to 30%, and waiting until 70 raises it by up to 24% for people with an FRA of 67. Full Retirement Age by Birth Year

Full retirement age (FRA), which the Social Security Administration (SSA) also calls normal retirement age, is the age at which you can collect 100% of your Social Security retirement benefit, known as your primary insurance amount. It depends only on the year you were born:

Two birthday rules can shift your FRA:

  • Born on January 1: use the full retirement age for the previous year. Someone born January 1, 1960, for example, uses the 1959 row (66 and 10 months).
  • Born on the 1st of any month: the SSA figures your benefit and FRA as if your birthday were in the previous month. You must also be 62 for the entire month to get benefits that month.

The SSA's FAQ confirms that FRA is 67 for people reaching age 62 in 2026. Survivor benefits for widows and widowers use a separate schedule that reaches 67 for people born in 1962 or later.

How Much Claiming Early Reduces Your Benefit

You can start retirement benefits as early as 62, but your monthly check is permanently reduced for each month you claim before FRA. Federal rules set the cut at 5/9 of 1% per month for the first 36 months early, and 5/12 of 1% for each additional month. With an FRA of 67, claiming at 62 is 60 months early: 36 months x 5/9% = 20%, plus 24 months x 5/12% = 10%, for a 30% reduction.

Spousal benefits are reduced faster: 25/36 of 1% per month for the first 36 months and 5/12 of 1% after that. The table below uses the SSA's example of a worker whose full benefit is $1,000 a month and a spouse whose maximum is $500 (50%):

For someone born in 1960 or later, a spouse who claims at 62 gets 32.5% of the worker's full benefit ($325 of $1,000) instead of 50%. To see what a 30% cut means in dollars at today's benefit levels, see the average Social Security check by claiming age.

Delayed Retirement Credits: Waiting Past FRA

If you wait past full retirement age, your benefit grows through delayed retirement credits. For anyone born in 1943 or later, the credit is 8% per year, or 2/3 of 1% per month. The increase stops at age 70, so there is no benefit to waiting longer.

One caution from the SSA: even if you delay Social Security, sign up for Medicare at 65. If you don't, your Medicare coverage may be delayed and cost more. Your full retirement age does not change Medicare: the SSA notes that the Medicare eligibility age remains 65.

Working Before Full Retirement Age: The Earnings Test

If you claim before FRA and keep working, the SSA withholds part of your benefit when your earnings pass an annual limit. The limit disappears starting the month you reach FRA.

Why Full Retirement Age Rose to 67

For decades, full retirement age was 65. The Social Security Amendments of 1983, signed on April 20, 1983, raised it in two stages to 67. Workers born in 1938 were the first group affected, and the age rose by two months per birth year until it reached 67 for people born in 1960 and later. The law kept the earliest claiming age at 62.

How Many People Claim Before FRA

Age 62 remains a popular choice. Among people awarded Social Security retired-worker benefits in 2025, 22.3% of men and 23.8% of women were 62, while 7.6% of men and 7.0% of women were 70 or older, according to the SSA's 2026 Annual Statistical Supplement. The average age at award was 65.2 for men and 65.0 for women. For how that compares with when people actually stop working, see the average retirement age.

How to Check Your FRA and Apply

  • Check your estimate: your Social Security Statement in a my Social Security account at ssa.gov includes a bar graph of your personalized benefit estimates at nine different claiming ages.
  • Apply up to 4 months early: the SSA says you can apply for retirement benefits up to 4 months before you want them to start. You can apply online, by phone at 1-800-772-1213 (TTY 1-800-325-0778), or at a local office by appointment.
  • Plan Medicare separately: the SSA suggests signing up for Medicare about 3 months before your 65th birthday.

The right claiming age depends on your health, savings, work plans and spouse, so there is no single answer. Our guides to how much money you need to retire and retirement savings by age can help you see how Social Security fits with your savings.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is my full retirement age if I was born in 1960?

Your full retirement age is 67 if you were born in 1960 or later. If you were born on January 1, 1960, the SSA uses the previous year's rule, so your full retirement age is 66 and 10 months.

What is the full retirement age for someone born in 1958?

The full retirement age for people born in 1958 is 66 and 8 months. Claiming at 62 is 56 months early and reduces a worker's benefit by about 28.33%.

How much is Social Security reduced if I claim at 62?

If your full retirement age is 67, claiming at 62 reduces your monthly benefit by 30%, so a $1,000 full benefit becomes $700. The reduction is permanent. For a full retirement age of 66, the cut at 62 is 25%.

How much more do you get by waiting until 70?

For people born in 1943 or later, delayed retirement credits add 8% per year after full retirement age. With a full retirement age of 67, waiting until 70 gives you 124% of your full benefit. The credits stop at 70.

Does full retirement age affect Medicare?

No. Medicare eligibility remains at 65 even though Social Security full retirement age is now 67 for people born in 1960 or later. The SSA advises signing up for Medicare at 65 even if you delay Social Security.
