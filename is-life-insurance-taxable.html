Is Life Insurance Taxable? What the IRS Says

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 8 min read

General information, not tax or legal advice. Based on IRS Publication 525 and other IRS guidance.

Usually not. The IRS says life insurance proceeds paid to you because the insured person died generally aren't taxable income. Taxes can still apply in a few cases: interest paid on the proceeds, cash value gains when you surrender a policy, employer coverage above $50,000, policies sold or transferred for value, and estate tax on very large estates. Here is how each one works.

Key Takeaways

  • Life insurance proceeds paid to a beneficiary because of the insured person's death are generally not taxable income, according to IRS Publication 525.
  • Interest paid on life insurance proceeds is taxable, including interest built into installment payments and interest on proceeds left on deposit with the insurer.
  • If you surrender a cash value policy, the amount you receive above your cost (generally the premiums you paid) is taxable, and the insurer sends a Form 1099-R.
  • Employers can provide up to $50,000 of group-term life insurance tax-free; the cost of coverage above $50,000 is added to your wages (IRS Publication 15-B).
  • Proceeds can be counted in the insured person's estate; the federal estate tax basic exclusion amount is $15,000,000 for deaths in 2026 (IRS).

The Short Answer: Death Benefits Are Usually Tax-Free

If you are the beneficiary of a life insurance policy and the insured person dies, the payout is generally not taxable income for you. IRS Publication 525 states that "life insurance proceeds paid to you because of the death of the insured person generally aren't taxable," and the IRS FAQ on life insurance proceeds says the same for proceeds a beneficiary receives because of the insured person's death.

That covers the most common situation: a spouse, child or other beneficiary receives a lump-sum death benefit. The exceptions below involve money on top of the death benefit, cash value inside a policy, or the way the policy was owned or transferred.

When Interest Makes Part of the Payout Taxable

The death benefit itself is tax-free, but any interest you receive on it is taxable. The IRS FAQ says to report the taxable amount based on the type of income document you receive, such as Form 1099-INT or Form 1099-R. Interest shows up in three common ways:

  • Delayed payment. If the insurer adds interest for the time between the death and the payout, that interest is taxable.
  • Installment payouts. If you take the benefit in installments, Publication 525 says to divide the amount held by the insurer by the number of installments. That part of each payment is tax-free. Anything above it is interest and goes into your income.
  • Proceeds left on deposit. If you leave the money with the insurer and it pays you interest only, that interest is taxable.

Publication 525 also keeps an older rule: if the insured was your spouse and died before October 23, 1986, you can exclude up to $1,000 a year of the interest included in installments.

Cash Value Policies: Surrenders, Loans and Withdrawals

Some permanent policies, such as whole life, build cash value, a savings component you can tap while you are alive. The rules in this section apply only to policies with cash value. If you are comparing the two, see our life insurance guide.

Surrendering a policy. If you cancel a policy for cash, Publication 525 says you must include in income any proceeds that are more than the cost of the policy. Your cost is generally the premiums you paid, reduced by amounts such as refunded premiums, rebates and dividends you received tax-free. You should receive a Form 1099-R showing the total proceeds and the taxable part. Endowment proceeds paid in a lump sum at maturity follow the same rule: taxable only above your cost.

Policy loans. A loan from a policy that is not a modified endowment contract is generally not treated as income, according to the Tax Facts reference published by ThinkAdvisor. The risk comes later: if the policy lapses or you surrender it while a loan is unpaid, the borrowed amount can become taxable at that point to the extent the cash value exceeds your basis in the contract.

Withdrawals. Northwestern Mutual explains that withdrawals from a policy that is not a modified endowment contract are usually not taxed if they are less than the premiums you paid.

Modified Endowment Contracts (MECs)

A modified endowment contract is a cash value policy that has been funded faster than tax law allows for normal life insurance treatment. Northwestern Mutual explains that each policy has a "7-pay" limit; if you pay in more than that amount in any of the policy's first seven years, it becomes a MEC.

Money you take out of a MEC while you are alive is taxed differently:

  • Withdrawals and loans come out of growth first, so they are taxable until all the gain is out.
  • The taxable part is ordinary income.
  • You may owe a 10% penalty on money taken out before age 59½, according to Northwestern Mutual.

If you plan to add large lump sums to a policy, ask your insurer whether the payment would exceed the 7-pay limit.

Selling or Transferring a Policy: The Transfer-for-Value Rule

The tax-free treatment can be lost if a policy changes hands for money or other value. Publication 525 says the exclusion does not apply if the policy was turned over to you for a price or acquired in a reportable policy sale. In that case, Western & Southern explains, the taxable amount is generally the death benefit minus what the buyer paid for the policy and the premiums the buyer paid afterward. It lists exceptions, including transfers to the insured, to a partner of the insured, and to a corporation where the insured is a shareholder or officer.

Accelerated Death Benefits

Some policies let you receive part of the death benefit early if the insured is seriously ill. Publication 525 says accelerated death benefits are fully excludable from income if the insured is terminally ill, meaning a physician has certified an illness or condition that can reasonably be expected to result in death within 24 months. Payments for a chronically ill person are also excludable within limits; Publication 525 sets a per diem limit of $420 a day for 2025.

Life Insurance Through Work: The $50,000 Rule

Employer-paid group-term life insurance is tax-free up to $50,000 of coverage. IRS Publication 15-B says the cost of coverage above $50,000, minus anything you paid toward it, must be included in your wages. That amount, often called imputed income, is subject to Social Security and Medicare tax; federal income tax withholding is optional for the employer, but the amount is still taxable income.

The cost is set by an IRS table (Table 2-2 in Publication 15-B), per $1,000 of excess coverage per month, based on your age on the last day of the tax year:

Example using the table: a 42-year-old with $150,000 of employer-paid coverage has $100,000 above the limit. That is 100 units × $0.10 × 12 months = $120 of imputed income for the year. Coverage on a spouse or dependent is a tax-free de minimis benefit if the face amount is not more than $2,000.

Estate Tax: When Proceeds Count Toward Your Estate

Income tax and estate tax are separate. Even when the beneficiary owes no income tax, the death benefit can be counted in the insured person's gross estate. The IRS instructions for Form 706 say the gross estate includes certain life insurance proceeds, even though they are payable to beneficiaries other than the estate.

Proceeds are included when they are payable to the estate, or when the person who died held incidents of ownership in the policy. Examples listed by the IRS include the power to change the beneficiary, to surrender or cancel the policy, or to pledge it for a loan. The executor asks each insurer for a Form 712, Life Insurance Statement, and attaches it to the return.

Federal estate tax is a concern mainly for large estates. For deaths in 2026, the basic exclusion amount is $15,000,000, up from $13,990,000 for 2025, according to the IRS. The annual gift tax exclusion stays at $19,000 per recipient for 2026.

For larger estates, one common approach is an irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT), which owns the policy instead of the insured. Timing matters: under the three-year rule, a policy you give away within three years of death is pulled back into your estate. Schwab notes that having the trust buy a new policy from the start avoids that rule. State estate and inheritance taxes have their own thresholds; check your state. For help with wills and trusts, see our Trust & Will review.

Practical checklist for beneficiaries: file the claim with the insurer, keep the insurer's payout statement, report any interest shown on a Form 1099-INT, and give the executor the policy details if the estate must file Form 706. Frequently Asked Questions

Do beneficiaries pay taxes on life insurance?

Generally no. The IRS says life insurance proceeds paid to you because of the death of the insured person generally aren't taxable income. Any interest paid on the proceeds is taxable.

Is the cash value of life insurance taxable?

It can be. If you surrender a cash value policy, the amount you receive above your cost, generally the premiums you paid, is taxable and reported on Form 1099-R.

Is employer-provided life insurance taxable?

The cost of up to $50,000 of employer-paid group-term life insurance is tax-free. The cost of coverage above $50,000 is added to your wages based on an IRS age table.

Is life insurance subject to estate tax?

It can be. Proceeds are included in the gross estate if they are payable to the estate or if the person who died held incidents of ownership in the policy. For deaths in 2026, the federal basic exclusion amount is $15,000,000.

Are life insurance policy loans taxable?

A loan from a policy that is not a modified endowment contract is generally not taxable. If the policy lapses or is surrendered with a loan outstanding, the loan can become taxable to the extent the cash value exceeds your basis.
