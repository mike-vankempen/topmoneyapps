Key Takeaways

  • This calculator projects your retirement savings from your age, current savings, monthly contributions and expected annual return, and shows the result in today's dollars after inflation.
  • It estimates retirement income with the 4% rule: withdraw about 4% of your savings in year one, then raise that amount with inflation each year.
  • A common target is 25 times your yearly spending in retirement; spending $60,000 a year takes about $1.5 million saved.
  • In 2026 you can contribute up to $24,500 to a 401(k) and $7,500 to an IRA.
  • Results assume a steady return every year. Real markets rise and fall, so treat the projection as an estimate, not a promise.

How This Retirement Calculator Works

The calculator grows your current savings and your monthly contributions at the annual return you choose, compounded monthly, until your retirement age. It then divides that balance by your inflation rate for each year until retirement to show what it would buy in today's dollars.

The monthly income figure applies the 4% rule to your projected balance: 4% a year, divided by 12. It is shown in future dollars, so compare it with the today's-dollar balance above it before deciding whether you're on track.

How Much Do You Need to Retire?

A widely used rule of thumb is 25 times your yearly spending. It comes from the 4% rule, published by financial planner William Bengen in 1994: if you withdraw 4% of your savings in your first year and raise that amount with inflation, the money historically lasted at least 30 years in most scenarios. Social Security, a pension or part-time income all lower the amount your savings need to cover. Our guide How Much Money Do You Need to Retire? walks through the math, and Retirement Savings by Age shows how others compare.

How to Close a Gap

  1. Capture your full employer match. A 401(k) match is the highest return available to most savers.
  2. Raise contributions with each raise. In 2026 you can put up to $24,500 in a 401(k) and $7,500 in an IRA; see 2026 contribution limits.
  3. Open an IRA if you don't have one. Compare low-cost providers in our best IRA apps guide.
  4. Work a few more years. Retiring later adds contributions and growth and shortens the years your savings must cover; move the retirement-age slider to see the effect.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much do I need to retire?
A common rule of thumb is 25 times your yearly spending in retirement. Retiring on $60,000 a year of spending would take about $1.5 million. The 25x rule comes from the 4% rule: 4% of $1.5 million is $60,000. Social Security and any pension lower how much you need to have saved.
What is the 4% rule?
The 4% rule says you can withdraw about 4% of your retirement savings in your first year, then raise that dollar amount with inflation each year, and historically the money lasted at least 30 years in most scenarios. Financial planner William Bengen published the research in 1994. It is a planning guideline, not a guarantee.
What annual return should I use in a retirement calculator?
Many planners use 5% to 7% a year for a diversified, stock-heavy portfolio, and less as you move toward bonds. This calculator also shows your balance in today's dollars using the inflation rate you set. If the return you enter is already after inflation, set inflation to 0%.
Does this retirement calculator include Social Security?
No. It projects only what you save and invest. To see your estimated Social Security benefit, create a free my Social Security account at ssa.gov, then subtract that income from what your savings need to cover.
How much can I contribute to retirement accounts in 2026?
In 2026 you can contribute up to $24,500 to a 401(k) and up to $7,500 to a Traditional or Roth IRA, with higher catch-up limits from age 50. Many employers also match part of your 401(k) contributions, which is the fastest way to raise your savings rate.
