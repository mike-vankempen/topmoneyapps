Sinking Funds: How to Save for Big Expenses

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 6 min read

General information, not financial advice. Rate data from the FDIC via FRED.

A sinking fund is money you set aside a little at a time for a specific, planned expense, such as holiday gifts, car repairs or a vacation. To size one, divide the total cost by the number of months until you need it: a $1,200 trip in 10 months means saving $120 a month. Unlike an emergency fund, which is for surprises, a sinking fund is meant to be spent on a known goal.

Key Takeaways

  • A sinking fund is money set aside regularly for a specific, planned future expense, such as a vacation, holiday gifts or car maintenance.
  • The monthly amount is the goal divided by the months until the expense: $1,000 for tires in 10 months is $100 a month (Synchrony example).
  • A sinking fund differs from an emergency fund: emergency money is for unexpected costs, while a sinking fund is for spending you can predict.
  • Common places to keep sinking funds are high-yield savings accounts, money market accounts, or a savings account with separate subaccounts or buckets.
  • The FDIC national average savings rate was 0.39% in November 2026, so where you keep a sinking fund affects how much interest it earns.

What Is a Sinking Fund?

Experian describes a sinking fund as money you set aside regularly every month to cover a future expense. SoFi calls it money earmarked for planned expenses that fall outside your regular budget. The idea is simple: instead of putting a $900 car repair or $750 of holiday gifts on a credit card, you spread the cost over the months before it arrives.

Sinking Fund vs. Emergency Fund

FeatureSinking fundEmergency fund
PurposePlanned, predictable costsUnexpected costs, such as job loss or a medical bill
TargetThe cost of one goalSeveral months of living expenses
Meant to be spent?Yes, on scheduleOnly in a real emergency
Number of fundsOften several, one per goalUsually one

You can have both. Synchrony notes that it's not an either/or choice, and Experian recommends keeping them separate so you don't dip into emergency money by accident. SoFi suggests an emergency fund covering three to six months of standard living expenses. To size yours, use our emergency fund calculator.

Common Sinking Fund Categories

The guides we reviewed list these as typical sinking funds. To find your own, SoFi suggests reviewing 6 to 12 months of spending for costs that come up once or twice a year.

  • Car: maintenance, repairs, tires, registration, or a down payment on the next car.
  • Home: repairs, appliances, furniture and property taxes if they are not escrowed.
  • Insurance: premiums you pay once or twice a year instead of monthly.
  • Holidays and gifts: birthdays, holidays and other gift-giving seasons.
  • Travel: vacations and trips to see family.
  • Pets: vet visits and pet care.
  • Life events: a wedding, a new baby, or a new laptop or phone.
  • Taxes: estimated taxes if you are self-employed (Experian).

Where to Keep Sinking Funds

The money should be safe and easy to reach on the date you need it, so most guides point to insured deposit accounts:

  • High-yield savings account: recommended by Synchrony and Chime. Look for no monthly maintenance fees.
  • Money market account: recommended by Capital One, Synchrony and SoFi. Some offer check-writing. See money market account vs. high-yield savings.
  • Savings buckets or subaccounts: SoFi suggests a savings account with subaccounts; Chime suggests a separate account per goal. Experian says one account can also work if you track each fund in a budgeting app, spreadsheet or paper ledger.
  • CDs: Capital One mentions a CD if you won't need the money soon, while SoFi says CDs are usually not the best place for a sinking fund.

The account you choose matters. The FDIC national average savings rate was 0.39% in November 2026, and SoFi notes that online banks tend to offer better rates. Compare current offers in our best high-yield savings accounts guide.

Tips to Make Sinking Funds Work

  • Automate it. Set automatic transfers from checking on payday so you don't have to remember (SoFi, Chime).
  • Treat contributions like bills. Experian's advice: don't skip them.
  • Name each fund. A label like "Tires, June" makes it less tempting to spend the money on something else.
  • Build them into your budget. Sinking funds fit any budgeting method. In zero-based budgeting they are their own line item, and many budgeting apps let you track goals. Compare methods in our best budgeting method guide.
  • Refill after spending. Once a fund pays for its goal, restart it for the next time the cost comes around.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a sinking fund?

A sinking fund is money you set aside regularly for a specific, planned expense, such as a vacation, holiday gifts or car repairs, so you can pay for it in cash when it comes due.

How do you calculate a sinking fund?

Divide the total cost by the number of months until you need the money. For example, $1,000 for new tires in 10 months means saving $100 a month.

Is a sinking fund the same as an emergency fund?

No. An emergency fund is for unexpected costs and is not meant to be spent otherwise. A sinking fund is for a known, planned expense and is meant to be spent on that goal.

Where should I keep my sinking funds?

Most guides suggest an insured high-yield savings account or money market account, either one account per goal or one account with subaccounts or buckets that track each fund.

How many sinking funds should I have?

There is no set number. Start with the irregular costs you know are coming in the next year, such as car maintenance, insurance premiums and holidays, and add funds as your budget allows.
