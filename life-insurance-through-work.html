Life Insurance Through Work: How Group Coverage Works and Its Limits

By Mike Van Kempen Updated November 2026 8 min read

General information, not insurance, tax or legal advice. Based on BLS, IRS and NAIC guidance.

Life insurance through work is group term life insurance your employer offers as a benefit. The basic amount is usually free to you and is often a flat dollar amount or a multiple of your salary; you can often buy more through payroll deductions. The main limits: the coverage is tied to your job, it may be less than your family needs, and employer-paid coverage above $50,000 creates taxable income. In March 2026, 59% of private industry workers had access to a workplace life insurance plan, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

Key Takeaways

  • In March 2026, 63% of civilian workers had access to employer life insurance and 62% participated; 98% of workers with access took it (BLS).
  • Access was 59% for private industry workers and 84% for state and local government workers in March 2026 (BLS).
  • According to LIMRA's 2026 Insurance Barometer Study, 47% of insured Americans have life insurance through work and 27% have only workplace coverage, with no individual policy.
  • The cost of employer-paid group term coverage above $50,000 must be added to your wages and is subject to Social Security and Medicare tax (IRS Publication 15-B).
  • When group coverage ends, insurers such as MetLife and Principal generally give you 31 days to convert it to an individual policy without a medical exam.

How Group Life Insurance Through Work Works

Group life insurance is one policy that an insurer issues to your employer and that covers many employees. Workplace coverage is typically group term insurance: it pays a lump sum to your beneficiary if you die while you are covered. The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) describes it simply: life insurance "provides a lump-sum payment to a designated beneficiary or beneficiaries of a deceased employee."

Most workers who are offered it take it. In March 2026, BLS found:

The take-up rate is the share of workers with access who are actually enrolled. It is high because basic coverage is usually paid by the employer. BLS put the average employer cost of life insurance at just $0.04 per hour worked in September 2022. For more coverage figures, see our life insurance statistics.

Basic Coverage: Flat Amount or Salary Multiple

Employer-paid coverage is called basic life insurance. Plans set the benefit in one of two main ways:

  • A multiple of your salary. In the most recent BLS breakdown (March 2011 data), 59% of full-time private industry workers with basic coverage were in plans paying a fixed multiple of annual earnings, and 27% had a multiple of at least 2.
  • A flat dollar amount. In the same data, 36% of full-time workers were in flat-amount plans, and the median benefit in those plans was $15,000.

Federal employees offer a clear example of a salary-based design. Under the Federal Employees' Group Life Insurance program (FEGLI), the Office of Personnel Management (OPM) says Basic coverage equals your annual basic pay rounded up to the next $1,000, plus $2,000. Optional coverage adds $10,000 (Option A), one to five multiples of pay (Option B), or family coverage of $5,000 for a spouse and $2,500 per child for each multiple (Option C).

Many employers also include accidental death and dismemberment (AD&D) coverage. MetLife says basic AD&D typically pays an accidental death benefit equal to your basic term life amount, but it does not pay for losses caused by illness. Treat AD&D as a supplement, not a substitute for life insurance.

Supplemental Life Insurance: Buying More at Work

Many plans let you buy supplemental (also called voluntary or optional) life insurance on top of the basic amount. Guardian describes it as additional coverage purchased at work, with premiums taken out of your pay automatically. Some key rules:

  • You pay the premium, usually through payroll deduction.
  • Spouse and child coverage is often available, but Guardian notes you typically must first buy supplemental coverage for yourself.
  • Guaranteed issue limits. Plans let you buy up to a set amount without health questions. MetLife defines this "guaranteed issue" amount as the maximum coverage you can get without providing medical information; above it, you must provide evidence of insurability (EOI), usually a health questionnaire. Limits vary by employer.

Open enrollment is usually the time to add or change supplemental coverage. Our open enrollment checklist covers the other benefits to review at the same time.

What Happens When You Leave Your Job

Group coverage generally ends when your employment ends. You usually have two ways to keep some protection, and the deadlines are short:

OptionWhat you getHealth questionsTypical deadline
PortabilityContinue group term coverage (and often AD&D) under a term policyMetLife: none for standard non-preferred ratesMetLife: generally 31 days; Principal: 60 days (31 in New York)
ConversionConvert to an individual permanent (whole life) policyNoneGenerally 31 days after coverage ends (MetLife, Principal)

Both options are priced on your age when you port or convert. MetLife says portability rates may change, while conversion rates stay the same for the life of the policy. Principal caps portable coverage at $500,000 for employees and $250,000 for spouses, and does not offer portability over age 70. Other insurers' rules differ, so check your certificate of coverage.

State law can add protections. Maryland's insurance regulator, for example, says that when an employer terminates a fully insured group plan, the insurer must give eligible employees at least 31 days to decide and must offer an individual policy regardless of health.

Don't miss the window: if you have a health condition that would make new coverage expensive or hard to get, conversion may be your only way to keep coverage without medical underwriting. Ask HR for the conversion and portability forms before your last day.

Taxes on Employer-Paid Coverage

The first $50,000 of employer-paid group term life coverage is tax-free. IRS Publication 15-B says the cost of coverage above $50,000, minus anything you paid toward it, must be included in your wages and is subject to Social Security and Medicare taxes. The cost is not the actual premium; it comes from an IRS table of monthly rates per $1,000 of coverage, from $0.05 under age 25 to $2.06 at 70 and older.

The IRS gives this example: a 45-year-old with $200,000 of coverage has $150,000 above the limit. At $0.15 per $1,000 per month, that is $270 a year; after the $100 he paid toward the coverage, $170 is added to his wages and shown on Form W-2 in box 12, code C. Coverage on a spouse or dependent is a tax-free benefit if the face amount is not more than $2,000. The death benefit itself is generally not taxable to your beneficiary; see Is Life Insurance Taxable?

Your Rights Under ERISA

Most group life plans at private employers are covered by the Employee Retirement Income Security Act (ERISA). The Department of Labor says Title I of ERISA covers most private sector employee benefit plans, including those providing death benefits, and gives participants the right to timely and fair claims processes. Your plan administrator must give you the summary plan description (SPD) free of charge; it explains your coverage and how to file a claim.

Keep your beneficiary designation current through your employer's benefits portal. If your beneficiary needs to make a claim later, our guide on how to file a life insurance claim walks through the steps.

The Limits of Workplace Coverage

Workplace coverage is a useful base, but regulators point to several gaps:

  • It may be too small. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) buyer's guide says that with free or low-cost employer coverage, "the death benefit usually is less than you need."
  • It is tied to your job. The NAIC notes you may not be able to take the coverage with you if you leave, and portable or converted coverage is repriced at your age.
  • It can shrink with age. Federal age discrimination rules (29 CFR 1625.10) note that it is "not uncommon" for coverage to stay level until a set age, frequently 65, and then be reduced, as long as the cut is justified by higher costs.
  • Many people rely on it alone. LIMRA's 2026 data show 27% of insured Americans have only workplace coverage, and 38% of adults say they need life insurance or more of it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much life insurance do you usually get through work?

It depends on the plan. Basic coverage is usually a flat dollar amount or a multiple of salary. In the latest BLS breakdown (March 2011 data), 59% of full-time private workers with coverage had a salary-multiple plan and the median flat benefit was $15,000. Check your benefits portal for your exact amount.

Is life insurance through work enough?

Often not on its own. The NAIC says the death benefit on free or low-cost employer coverage is usually less than you need, and LIMRA reports that 27% of insured Americans have only workplace coverage. Compare your coverage with an estimate of your family's needs.

What happens to my work life insurance when I quit?

Group coverage generally ends with your job. Many plans let you port the term coverage or convert it to an individual whole life policy without a medical exam, usually within about 31 days after coverage ends. Rates are based on your age at that time.

Is employer-paid life insurance taxable?

The first $50,000 of employer-paid group term coverage is tax-free. The IRS-table cost of coverage above $50,000, minus what you paid, is added to your wages and is subject to Social Security and Medicare tax.

What is supplemental life insurance at work?

It is extra coverage you buy through your employer, usually paid by payroll deduction. Amounts above the plan's guaranteed issue limit require evidence of insurability, such as a health questionnaire.
